## The decisions this raises — for the candidate and supervisor, not for the instrument

Almost none of what follows is a software defect. They are choices about the DESIGN of the study,
each with large downstream consequences (session length, sample size, the feasibility gate), and
each resting on citations that are not yet verified. They are recorded here so the decision is
taken deliberately rather than by default. Nothing in the instrument has been changed on the
strength of them.

The four that recur across independent searches, and matter most:

1. **Exposure duration versus the factorial.** Several searches independently reported that
   incomplete blinking accumulates over tens of minutes rather than switching on, and that no
   retrieved study demonstrates a detectable change at ~3 minutes. The current design buys ten
   conditions per sitting by making each exposure short. That trade cannot be had both ways:
   either the exposure lengthens and the within-sitting factorial shrinks, or the primary outcome
   moves to a measure that responds on this timescale. This is the single biggest question.

2. **Ambient illumination corrupts the measurement channel it is being compared across.** The
   between-sitting IV is room light; the sensor is a visible-light camera whose exposure, gain and
   achieved frame rate all change with room light. A difference between 10 lux and 150 lux could
   be biology or could be the camera. Mitigations range from fixed camera exposure and frame rate
   in software (cheap, partial) to a constant near-infrared illuminator (expensive, decisive).
   The instrument already exports `effective_fps`, `mean_face_luma` and `lighting_quality` per
   condition, so the confound is at least measurable.

3. **The EAR cut-points are unvalidated.** 0.75 to register and 0.60 for complete appear in no
   retrieved validation study, and published operationalisations differ. The synopsis already
   calls them provisional pending the annotation subsample — which is the right posture, and is
   now actually collectable, since the video-consent path was unreachable until this session.

4. **The CVS-Q may not be able to move within one sitting.** It is a period-recall instrument;
   its floor behaviour and the absence of a published minimal detectable change were both raised.
   The per-condition 5-item fatigue scale, not the CVS-Q, is what actually carries the
   within-sitting hypotheses.

What WAS changed in the instrument, because it is a measurement defect either way and independent
of which design is chosen: the incomplete-blink ratio now carries its own frame-rate
qualification (`fps_adequate_for_ratio`, 30 fps). It previously had none — only the duration
tiers were gated, at 25 fps — so a condition captured at 12 fps entered the analysis looking
identical to one captured at 30. Undersampling biases the sampled minimum EAR upward and so
inflates the ratio; it is a directional bias, not symmetric noise. Conditions are FLAGGED, never
dropped, precisely because frame rate covaries with ambient illumination.

## The incomplete-blink ratio measured during a reading task is a valid, sensitive primary outcome for display-induced visual fatigue / computer vision syndrome.

**Verdict: `partially_supported`** (unverified evidence)

### What the searches reported

The construct is real and better-chosen than blink rate, but the specific operationalisation in this protocol — a ~180 s reading exposure, an unvalidated EAR cut-point, and an uncontrolled device frame rate under a between-sitting illumination manipulation — is not supported by the literature, and two of those choices are actively contradicted by it.

WHAT IS WELL SUPPORTED. Incomplete blinking is an established correlate of tear-film instability and evaporative dry eye, and it consistently outperforms blink rate. In the largest and most rigorous study retrieved (Wang et al. 2025, investigator-masked prospective registry, n=453), a higher proportion of incomplete blinking predicted TFOS DEWS II dry eye diagnosis (OR 1.12 per 10% increase, 95% CI 1.05-1.19, p<0.001; Youden-optimal threshold >=40% incomplete blinking) and was associated with poorer symptomology, tear stability, staining, lipid layer thickness, meibography and meibum quality (all p<=0.03) — while blink RATE showed no significant association with any ocular surface parameter (all p>0.10). This replicates Wang et al. 2018 (n=154: incomplete blinking OR 2.2 for dry eye; blink frequency correlated with nothing) and Chidi-Egboka et al. 2023 (n=24, in-situ eye tracking: blink rate and inter-blink interval unassociated with any symptom or sign). Mechanistically, Hirota et al. 2013 showed tear film stays stable despite falling blink rate provided blinks are complete, and TFOS DEWS III (2025) now formally subclassifies dry eye to include an "eyelid anomalies (blink/lid closure)" aetiological driver. So the protocol's choice of incomplete-blink ratio OVER blink rate is defensible and current. In the CVS-specific setting, Portello, Rosenfield & Chu 2013 (15-min desktop reading, n=21) found the percentage of incomplete blinks correlated positively with post-task symptom score (p=0.002), and Golebiowski et al. 2019 found the increase in incomplete blinks over 60 min of smartphone reading tracked worsening ocular surface symptoms (rho=-0.65, p=0.02).

WHAT IS NOT SUPPORTED — EXPOSURE DURATION. This is the most serious problem. Every study that has actually moved incomplete blinking used tens of minutes, and the measure builds progressively rather than switching on. Golebiowski et al. 2019 reported incomplete blinks rising from a median of 6/min at 1 minute to 15/min at 60 minutes (p=0.0049) — at 3 minutes the value is essentially baseline. Hirota et al. 2013 traced a cubic time course across 60 min with inflections near 30 and 50 min. Lin et al. 2025 — the closest analogue to this protocol (ambient illuminance x screen brightness, mobile phone reading, n=30) — used 30-minute blocks and found incomplete blink rate rising WITHIN each block. Redondo et al. 2025 likewise used 30-minute blocks. By contrast, Chidi-Egboka et al. 2023 showed blink RATE and inter-blink interval shift fully within the first minute and then stay flat. The literature therefore says the opposite of what the protocol assumes: the fast-moving measure is the one being demoted to secondary, and the slow-accumulating measure is being made primary on a 180 s exposure. No retrieved study demonstrates a detectable incomplete-blink change at ~3 minutes.

WHAT IS NOT SUPPORTED — MEASUREMENT. Zheng et al. 2022 (TVST, deep-learning blink analysis, 50 dry eye + 50 controls) explicitly tested sampling rate and recommended >=30 fps, with 8 fps giving lower sensitivity and accuracy. Wolter et al. 2025 (arXiv preprint, not peer-reviewed) quantified this: blink detection accuracy 51% at 10 Hz versus 95% at 30 Hz, and drowsiness classifiers trained across mixed frame rates collapsed (46-69% versus 77-98% at matched rates). The same preprint showed EAR specifically is viewpoint-dependent, with substantially higher variance across head yaw/pitch than a 3D eyelid-angle metric — a direct hazard for a protocol that lets head pose float and measures it only as an outcome. Separately, Navascues-Cornago et al. 2020 found blink parameters differ significantly between white-light and infrared illumination (palpebral aperture, blink rate, closing and opening speed, duration; all p<=0.04), which matters because this protocol's between-sitting factor IS ambient illumination and the sensor is a visible-light camera whose exposure and achieved frame rate will differ between 10 lux and 150 lux. The EAR cut-points (0.75 to register, 0.60 for complete) appear in no retrieved validation study; published definitions are heterogeneous, and Ren et al. 2024 showed two commercial devices measuring the same construct disagree materially (LipiView gave higher incomplete-blink counts and rates than a deep-learning Keratograph 5M pipeline, p<=0.006). Nyström et al. 2024 makes the general point that blink parameters shift systematically with the signal source used to derive them.

RELIABILITY AND MDC — LARGELY ABSENT. Ren et al. 2024 reported intraclass correlations for incomplete-blink RATE of only 0.633 (deep-learning K5M) and 0.589 (LipiView) on repeated measurement — moderate at best, and below the >=0.75 normally expected of a primary endpoint; the same study's ICCs were much better for blink count (0.841) than for the ratio. Chidi-Egboka et al. 2023 reported a coefficient of repeatability of +/-12.4 blinks/min against a mean of 10.7 — repeatability wider than the mean itself. I found NO study reporting test-retest reliability or minimum detectable change for incomplete-blink ratio measured by a front-facing consumer camera during reading. That gap is itself a finding: the protocol proposes as its primary endpoint a quantity whose measurement error has never been characterised on the instrument being used.

WITHIN-PERSON STABILITY AND CEILING. Between-subject variance is enormous relative to any plausible condition effect. Portello et al. 2013 found incomplete-blink percentages spanning 0.9-56.5% with mean 16.1% and SD 15.7 (SD approximately equal to the mean). Shew et al. 2022 found 58-83% incomplete blinks in healthy young adults depending on eyelid crease morphology, concluding incomplete blinking "predominates in habitual blinking" regardless of lid type. Yang et al. 2023 (n=1119 myopic children) reported mean partial-blink rate 0.81, with 88.1% exceeding a 0.4 threshold. This is directly relevant to an Assam cohort: Shew et al. 2022 and Kim, Wang & Craig 2018 both document markedly higher incomplete blinking in Asian participants and single-eyelid morphology (83% in the single-lid group). A cohort already sitting near 0.8 leaves little headroom for a display manipulation to move a proportion.

EVIDENCE THAT ACTIVELY CONTRADICTS THE CLAIM. Vigo et al. 2020 (100 dry eye, 100 matched controls) found automated blinking analysis discriminated dry eye at AUC 0.533 (sensitivity 0.48, specificity 0.59) — essentially chance — against NIBUT at 0.841, and found no significant group difference in blinking at all. Talens-Estarelles et al. 2022 found that a 2-week 20-20-20 intervention significantly improved digital eye strain and dry eye SYMPTOMS while producing no change in blink rate, incomplete blinking, or any other ocular surface or tear film sign (all p>=0.089) — symptoms and blink signs dissociate. Even the closest supporting analogue is weak: Lin et al. 2025 found the correlation between incomplete-blink frequency and visual fatigue score was only r=0.29 (p=0.03), roughly 8% shared variance, and that paper has since drawn a published Letter to the Editor (Erdinest & Bura) with an authors' Reply, so it is contested in print.

BOTTOM LINE. "Valid" is partially supported — incomplete blinking is a legitimate, currently endorsed marker of blink/lid-closure dysfunction and evaporative dry eye, and is the right choice relative to blink rate. "Sensitive primary outcome" is not supported for this protocol as written: the exposure is roughly an order of magnitude shorter than any study that has moved the measure, the reliability of the ratio is moderate at best even on dedicated clinical instruments, its diagnostic discrimination is contested, and the front-camera EAR implementation is confounded with the study's own illumination factor through frame rate and head pose.

### Implications raised for this protocol

**must fix**

- **The ~180 s reading exposure is roughly an order of magnitude shorter than any study that has successfully moved incomplete blinking. Golebiowski et al. 2019 measured a median of 6 incomplete blinks/min at 1 minute rising to 15/min at 60 minutes - the effect accumulates, so at 3 minutes the measure is still at baseline. Hirota et al. 2013 found a cubic time course over 60 min with inflections near 30 and 50 min. Lin et al. 2025 and Redondo et al. 2025, the two closest design analogues, both used 30-minute blocks. The one measure that is proven to shift within 60 seconds (Chidi-Egboka et al. 2023) is blink RATE - which this protocol has demoted to secondary.**
  Do not run incomplete-blink ratio as primary on a 180 s exposure. Choose one of two coherent designs. (A) Keep incomplete-blink ratio as primary and lengthen exposure to at least 20-30 min per condition to match Lin 2025 - which forces the within-sitting factorial down from 10 conditions to at most 2-4 (e.g. 2 polarities x 2 text colours), moving text colour to a between-sitting or separate-substudy factor. (B) Keep the 10-condition Williams square and 180 s exposures, and re-designate the primary outcome as blink rate / inter-blink interval, which Chidi-Egboka 2023 showed changes within 1 minute and remains stable thereafter - while stating plainly that those measures showed no association with ocular surface symptoms or signs in that same study, so the primary would be a display-response measure rather than a fatigue measure. Option A is the scientifically stronger choice; option B preserves the factorial at the cost of the fatigue claim. A hybrid that keeps both the 10-condition square and the incomplete-blink primary is not defensible.

- **The blink-completeness metrics are gated only at ~25 fps and otherwise run at 'whatever frame rate the device sustains'. Zheng et al. 2022 explicitly tested this and recommends >=30 fps, with 8 fps degrading sensitivity and accuracy. Wolter et al. 2025 measured 51% blink-detection accuracy at 10 Hz versus 95% at 30 Hz. Worse, completeness is derived from the EAR MINIMUM within a blink; a low frame rate systematically misses the deepest frame, raising the recorded minimum EAR and misclassifying complete blinks as incomplete - a directional upward bias on the primary outcome. Most dangerously, camera auto-exposure lengthens integration time in dim light, so the achieved frame rate will be systematically LOWER in the 10 lux sitting than the 150 lux sitting - which means the between-sitting illumination effect and the measurement bias point in the same direction and are inseparable.**
  Hard-gate ALL blink-completeness metrics (not just duration-based ones) at >=30 fps, citing Zheng et al. 2022; discard conditions that fall below it rather than analysing them. Lock the camera to a fixed exposure time and fixed frame rate in software across both sittings so that 10 lux and 150 lux cannot differ in sampling. Log achieved fps for every condition, report the fps distribution by illumination level, and pre-register a test that mean fps does not differ between the two sittings; if it does, the illumination main effect on incomplete-blink ratio is uninterpretable. Add achieved fps as a covariate in the primary model regardless.

- **The between-sitting manipulation is ambient illumination and the instrument is a visible-light front camera, so illumination changes both the biology and the measurement. Navascues-Cornago et al. 2020 found significant differences in palpebral aperture height (p<0.0001), blink rate (p=0.04), closing speed (p=0.009), opening speed (p<0.0001) and blink duration (p=0.0003) between white-light and infrared illumination of the same eyes. Landmark localisation accuracy also degrades with image noise at 10 lux, and Nystrom et al. 2024 showed blink metrics shift systematically with data quality.**
  Add a fixed, constant near-infrared illuminator and use IR capture for the eye measurement so the optical conditions at the sensor are identical at 10 lux and 150 lux, decoupling the experimental factor from the measurement channel. If IR hardware is not feasible on the tablet, run a mandatory instrument-bias calibration: at both lux levels record each participant holding a static, deliberately held-open gaze for 20-30 s and a set of instructed full closures, and use the resulting EAR distributions to quantify and correct the illumination-dependent measurement shift. Report that bias term in the thesis; do not assume it is zero.

- **The EAR cut-points (register at <=0.75 of open-eye baseline, complete at <=0.60) appear in no retrieved validation study. Published operationalisations are heterogeneous and non-equivalent: Wang et al. 2025's clinically anchored threshold is >=40% incomplete blinking on an observer scale; Zheng et al. 2022 uses relative interpalpebral height from segmented images; Ren et al. 2024 showed LipiView and a deep-learning Keratograph pipeline give significantly different incomplete-blink counts and rates on the same eyes (p<=0.006). Nystrom et al. 2024 shows blink parameters shift with the derivation signal itself.**
  Run and pre-register a validation substudy before the main data collection: in a subsample of roughly 20-30 participants, record blink behaviour simultaneously with the tablet front camera and a reference standard (high-speed video or a masked clinical grader scoring completeness), and derive the EAR completeness cut-point empirically against that reference rather than asserting 0.60. Report sensitivity/specificity of the chosen cut-point. In the main analysis, report the primary result across a pre-specified grid of cut-points (e.g. 0.50, 0.55, 0.60, 0.65, 0.70) as a robustness check, and state explicitly in the synopsis that this protocol's EAR-defined incomplete-blink ratio is not numerically interchangeable with LipiView PBR, Keratograph IB proportion, or observer-graded incomplete blinking.

- **No reliability or minimum-detectable-change figure exists for the proposed measurement. The best available comparators are discouraging: Ren et al. 2024 found ICC of only 0.633 and 0.589 for incomplete-blink RATE on dedicated clinical devices - the ratio was the least reliable of the three parameters tested and falls below the >=0.75 conventionally required of a primary endpoint - and Chidi-Egboka et al. 2023 reported a repeatability coefficient of +/-12.4 blinks/min against a mean of 10.7. The target n=130 appears to have been chosen without an MDC anchor.**
  Build a repeatability arm into the sitting: repeat one reference condition (e.g. positive polarity / black text) twice within each sitting at matched, pre-specified sequence positions, and compute ICC and MDC95 for the incomplete-blink ratio in this exact setup. Report those figures before any condition contrast. Re-derive the sample size from the observed MDC and from the Lin et al. 2025 effect magnitude (r=0.29 for incomplete-blink frequency vs fatigue), not from an assumed large effect - and state in the synopsis that if MDC95 exceeds the smallest condition difference of interest, the study is descriptive on this endpoint rather than confirmatory.

- **Between-subject variance in incomplete blinking swamps plausible condition effects, and this cohort is at high risk of a ceiling. Portello et al. 2013: range 0.9-56.5%, mean 16.1%, SD 15.7. Shew et al. 2022: 83+/-22% incomplete in single-eyelid-crease participants, 58-59% in partial and double crease, in a cohort 52% East Asian. Yang et al. 2023 (n=1119): mean partial blink rate 0.81 with 88.1% above 0.4. Kim, Wang & Craig 2018 independently confirms significantly higher incomplete blinking in Asian participants (both lid morphologies, p<0.001). An Assam student cohort may sit near 0.7-0.85 at baseline, leaving almost no headroom for a display manipulation to raise the proportion.**
  Three concrete changes. (1) Record upper eyelid crease morphology (single / partial / double) per participant at the profile stage and enter it as a covariate and stratification variable - Shew et al. 2022 gives the grading scheme. (2) Make the primary endpoint a within-subject CHANGE from that participant's own pre-task baseline incomplete-blink ratio, with participant as a random intercept, rather than the absolute ratio - otherwise the model is dominated by lid anatomy. (3) Pre-specify a ceiling-aware analysis: model the ratio with beta regression or on the logit scale rather than as a raw proportion, and report the observed baseline distribution early (after roughly the first 30 participants) so that a floor/ceiling problem is detected before the full 130 are run.

- **The washout design is built for chromatic/photopic adaptation, not for ocular-surface recovery. A 60 s neutral grey field (120 s on polarity switch) has no support in the retrieved literature as sufficient for blink-completeness recovery. Hirota et al. 2013 showed the incomplete-blink rate and ring break-up time were still fluctuating across 15-minute measurement intervals within a 60-min task, and Golebiowski et al. 2019 showed monotone accumulation across an hour. If incomplete blinking accumulates monotonically across the 10-condition sitting, the Williams square controls first-order carryover only under an additivity assumption that is violated, and condition effects become confounded with position in sequence.**
  Do not claim the grey field washes out the ocular-surface state - describe it accurately as a chromatic/luminance adaptation interval only, and say so in the methods. Add sequence position (1-10) as a fixed effect in the primary model and pre-register an explicit test for a linear and quadratic time-on-task trend in incomplete-blink ratio across the sitting; report it as a primary sensitivity analysis. If a significant monotone trend is present, state that within-sitting condition contrasts on this endpoint are confounded and interpret only the between-sitting illumination contrast, where the 48-72 h gap does provide a genuine washout.

**should fix**

- **EAR is viewpoint-dependent. Wolter et al. 2025 showed EAR varies substantially with head yaw and pitch at a FIXED true eyelid opening, because it relies on 2D landmark distances that distort under rotation. This protocol already computes head pose but treats it as an outcome/descriptor, not as a confounder on the primary measure - yet negative-polarity and 10 lux conditions plausibly change viewing posture and tablet holding angle.**
  Enter head pitch and yaw as time-varying covariates in the incomplete-blink model, and report a sensitivity analysis restricted to frames within a pre-specified head-pose window (e.g. +/-15 degrees yaw and pitch from the participant's calibration pose). Report whether mean head pose differs by polarity or by illumination level; if it does, the EAR-derived primary outcome is partly a posture measure. Also consider deriving completeness from a pose-normalised metric using the MediaPipe 3D landmarks rather than raw 2D EAR - the same source describes a 3D eyelid-angle alternative with lower viewpoint variance.

- **The claim as written in the synopsis - 'valid, sensitive primary outcome for display-induced visual fatigue / computer vision syndrome' - overstates what the literature supports. Vigo et al. 2020 found automated blinking analysis discriminated dry eye at AUC 0.533 (sensitivity 0.48, specificity 0.59), essentially chance, with no significant group difference. TFOS DEWS III (2025) positions blink/lid-closure assessment as an aetiological SUBCLASSIFICATION tool, not as an outcome measure. Jie et al. 2019 explicitly calls incomplete blinking an 'additive' measure for mild-to-moderate dry eye.**
  Rewrite the endpoint justification to claim only what is supported: incomplete-blink ratio is a consensus-recognised marker of blink/lid-closure dysfunction and a predictor of evaporative dry eye risk (Wang et al. 2025, OR 1.12 per 10%; Wang et al. 2018, OR 2.2), and it outperforms blink rate, which shows no association with ocular surface signs in three independent datasets. Frame the endpoint as a within-subject display-condition contrast in an objective ocular-behaviour marker, not as a diagnostic marker for CVS. Cite Vigo et al. 2020 in the limitations as the counter-evidence a reviewer will otherwise raise.

- **The protocol assumes the primary (incomplete-blink ratio) and the key secondary (CVS-Q change from baseline) index the same underlying construct. Talens-Estarelles et al. 2022 found the opposite: a 2-week 20-20-20 intervention significantly improved digital eye strain and dry eye symptoms while blink rate, incomplete blinking, and every ocular surface and tear film sign remained unchanged (all p>=0.089). Symptoms and blink signs dissociate.**
  State explicitly in the analysis plan that the objective blink endpoint and the subjective CVS-Q/fatigue endpoints are not interchangeable and are not expected to converge, cite Talens-Estarelles et al. 2022 for the dissociation, and pre-specify that a null result on one does not invalidate the other. Do not build a hypothesis that requires them to agree, and do not use CVS-Q change as a validation criterion for the blink measure.

- **Power appears not to be anchored to a published effect size for this manipulation. The only directly comparable figure is Lin et al. 2025, where incomplete-blink frequency correlated with visual fatigue score at r=0.29 (p=0.03) - about 8% shared variance - after 30 minutes of exposure, i.e. an exposure ten times longer than this protocol's.**
  Recompute power against r approximately 0.29 (or the equivalent standardised within-subject mean difference from Lin et al. 2025's Group 4 vs Group 1 contrast), and state that this estimate derives from a 30-minute exposure so it is an upper bound for a 180 s exposure. n=130 is likely adequate for a correlation of that size but the synopsis must show the calculation and the source. Also cite the Letter to the Editor (Erdinest & Bura, doi 10.1016/j.clae.2026.102618) and the authors' Reply alongside Lin et al. 2025, since the paper is contested in print.

- **The per-condition task battery varies information-presentation rate and cognitive load across the sequence (reading, then MCQs, ratings, a 5-item scale, a timed visual search, then a 32-trial go/no-go), and these are known to alter blink behaviour independently of display parameters. Cardona et al. 2011 found a significantly larger percentage of incomplete blinks during more dynamic visual display tasks (chi-square=34.00, p<0.001) with blink rate falling to 1/3 of baseline. Redondo et al. 2025 found cognitive load independently raised tear IL-8 and TNF-beta during digital reading.**
  Because the primary outcome is defined as the incomplete-blink ratio DURING THE READING TASK specifically, make that windowing explicit and mechanical in the analysis code: extract only frames within the reading epoch, exclude the first 10 s after page onset and any epoch containing a page turn, and report the number of blinks contributing to each participant-condition ratio (a 180 s reading epoch at a task blink rate near 10.7/min yields only about 32 blinks, so the ratio's binomial standard error alone is roughly 8-9 percentage points - report this). Verify that comprehension difficulty is matched across the ten reading passages, since cognitive load is a documented confound on both blink completeness and the inflammatory markers of digital eye strain.

### What the searches reported

The claim splits into three parts, and the literature treats them very differently.

(1) DOES EAR DETECT BLINKS AT ALL? Yes, moderately well. Soukupová & Čech's 2016 EAR formulation is the foundational method and remains the reference implementation. Against manual/expert ground truth, EAR-family detectors on consumer cameras land in the low-to-mid 90s for accuracy: Ersoy et al. (2026) report 91.7% (generalised threshold) and 93.2% (personalised threshold) for eye-state classification from MediaPipe Face Mesh, against 99.1% for a CNN on the same data — i.e. EAR is the weakest of the methods they tested. Schmidt et al. (2018, Behavior Research Methods) is the best-powered camera-vs-EOG comparison retrieved (14 manual + 16 automated driving sessions, ~2h45 each, 24 reference groups, expert frame-level eyelid-closure definitions): maximum correct detection 94% in alert/manual phases, falling by ≥2% when drowsy and ≥9% in automated phases. So ~90-94% is the realistic ceiling for blink OCCURRENCE, and it degrades with state and condition.

(2) ARE THRESHOLDS ABSOLUTE OR BASELINE-NORMALISED? Both appear, and normalisation is the better-supported choice. Absolute thresholds are common — Dewi et al. (2022, Electronics) optimised an absolute EAR threshold to 0.18 and showed AUC/accuracy degrade as the threshold is raised — but absolute thresholds are explicitly criticised for failing to generalise across facial geometry and illumination. Personalised baselines are the current direction of travel, and the protocol's 0.75 registration figure has a direct published precedent: Ersoy et al. (2026) calibrate each driver for 5 s and set the eye-closure threshold at exactly 75% of that person's baseline EAR. Ru (2026) instead estimates baseline from 90 open-eye frames and sets the threshold at 50% of baseline, reporting F1 gains of 4.4-15.3 points over conventional EAR under occlusion and inter-subject variability. So 0.75-of-baseline is defensible for REGISTRATION, though it sits at the permissive end of published practice and will admit small lid movements, twitches and squints into the denominator of the incomplete-blink ratio.

(3) DOES A 0.60-OF-BASELINE CUT VALIDLY SEPARATE COMPLETE FROM INCOMPLETE BLINKS? This is the load-bearing half of the claim, and I found NO published validation of it — not for MediaPipe FaceMesh, not for any webcam EAR, not at 0.60 or any other baseline fraction. Every study I retrieved that classifies complete versus incomplete blinks against a reference standard uses a different signal entirely: Nousias et al. (2022, IEEE JBHI) use near-infrared illumination, high-resolution zoomed imaging and DeepLabv3+ segmentation of iris and eyelid, thresholding palpebral fissure height divided by iris diameter, validated against three independent expert graders with senior adjudication (15 participants) — F1 95.3% for complete blinks but only 80.9% for INCOMPLETE blinks, despite outperforming all three human experts. Zheng et al. (2022, TVST; 50 DED + 50 controls) use a Keratograph 5M and a deep learning model on relative interpalpebral height. Navascues-Cornago et al. (2020) use high-speed infrared palpebral aperture height. Wang et al. (2025, CLAE; n=453) use a masked clinical observer. The nearest EAR-based work on partial blinks is Zhang & Echegoyen (2023, J Clin Med), an iPhone app on 10 DED patients and 10 controls reporting "excellent agreement with manual counts" but publishing no sensitivity, specificity, or the threshold values themselves; and Ayoub et al. (2024), which detects partial blinks with a Lacrimal Aspect Ratio at absolute (not baseline-normalised) thresholds of -2.4/-2.6/-2.9 with no clinical reference standard. Two things follow: incomplete blinks are the demonstrably harder class even with far better instrumentation (F1 0.81 vs 0.95 in Nousias), and EAR-with-a-baseline-fraction has never been shown to recover it.

CONTRADICTING EVIDENCE. Four independent lines cut against the claim as stated in this protocol's setting. (a) VIEWPOINT. Wolter et al. (2025) benchmark EAR against a 3D Eyelid Angle metric computed from the same MediaPipe Face Mesh and show EAR "is inherently viewpoint dependent, as it relies on 2D landmark distances that distort under head rotation", with markedly higher variance across camera sweeps; a metric that is unstable under head pose cannot carry a fixed fractional amplitude cut. (b) DOWNGAZE. Culemann et al. (2026, Behavior Research Methods, n=17) show vertical eye orientation systematically influences ALL blink parameters: eye openness at blink onset and closing amplitude both DECREASE with downward eye orientation, and blink duration rises 8% (eyelid-based) to 32% (pupil-based) from upward to downward gaze. Reading a tablet is a downgaze task with gaze migrating down each page — so both the numerator and the denominator of a baseline-normalised EAR ratio move with the reading position. (c) LOW LIGHT. Zhou (2022, IEEE TOCS) tested MediaPipe blink detection directly: average success rate 95.9% (right) / 91.2% (left) under adequate light collapsed to 39.7% / 48.8% under insufficient light with no image enhancement (recovering to 84% / 92.7% only after Zero-DCE low-light enhancement). Chhimpa et al. (2024, IEEE Access) independently report their MediaPipe Face Mesh pipeline shows "a high level of sensitivity to low light conditions" and "a moderate reaction to glasses", and Yıldız et al. (2021) found halving ambient light dropped their webcam gaze accuracy from 99.54% to 94.74%. (d) ILLUMINATION CHANGES THE MEASURAND, NOT JUST THE MEASUREMENT. Navascues-Cornago et al. (2020, Ophthalmic Physiol Opt) found white-light versus infrared illumination produced significant differences in palpebral aperture height (p<0.0001), blink rate (p=0.04), closing speed (p=0.009), opening speed (p<0.0001) and blink duration (p=0.0003) in the same subjects. Ambient illumination is this protocol's between-sitting independent variable, so at 10 lux the measurement error and the effect of interest are aliased.

FRAME RATE. The evidence here is more favourable than the protocol's own gating assumes, but points to a higher floor. Navascues-Cornago et al. (2026, The Ocular Surface) recorded 20 participants (spontaneous) plus 10 (reflex) at 500 fps and downsampled to 250/100/50/25 fps with Bland-Altman analysis: blink AMPLITUDE and DURATION showed minimal bias and narrow limits of agreement at ALL frame rates including 25 fps, whereas maximum velocities were markedly underestimated below 250 fps and average closing velocity needed 100-250 fps. So amplitude — which is what blink completeness actually is — survives 25 fps, and the protocol's rule of gating DURATION measures below 25 fps is aimed at the wrong quantity. However, Zheng et al. (2022) compared 30 fps against 8 fps for incomplete-blink parameters specifically and found 30 fps gave higher sensitivity and accuracy, recommending ≥30 fps. Against Nakamura et al.'s (2008) 1 kHz kinematics — down-phase ~100 ms, up-phase ~220 ms, total ~320 ms — 30 fps gives roughly three frames in the closing phase and only one to three frames near the EAR minimum, which is where the complete/incomplete decision is made.

IS MEDIAPIPE SPECIFICALLY VALIDATED? No. Everything I retrieved that pairs MediaPipe FaceMesh with EAR is engineering work on driver drowsiness, gaze interfaces, or unrefereed preprints — none validated against a high-speed-video, EOG, infrared eye-tracking or expert frame-by-frame ophthalmic reference standard for blink completeness. The only clinical EAR study on eyelid amplitude is Schuhmann et al. (2024, Sci Rep; 30 facial palsy patients + 30 matched controls, smartphone video), which uses MINIMAL EAR as "minimal visible eye surface during blinking" and shows it discriminates paretic from non-paretic sides — useful precedent that an EAR trough carries amplitude information, but it validates a group difference, not a per-blink classification threshold.

OTHER FAILURE MODES. Nousias et al. (2022) note their system tolerated "glare and reflections from the spectacles, or face obstruction by facemasks" — but that robustness came from near-IR segmentation, not from EAR. On eyelid morphology, Gao et al. (2024, Quant Imaging Med Surg; 46 Asian, 101 Caucasian adults aged 18-30, 3D anthropometry) found significant ethnic differences in palpebral fissure height, iris diameter, palpebral fissure index, lateral canthal angle and canthal tilt, with less prominent double-eyelid folds in the Chinese group. Because EAR is a height-to-width ratio, the palpebral fissure index directly sets its dynamic range: baseline normalisation rescales the OPEN end but does not make a 0.60 fraction correspond to the same physical lid gap at the CLOSED end across morphologies. Finally, Culemann et al. (2023) and Nyström et al. (2024) both show blink metrics depend systematically on device, algorithm and blink definition (Nyström: F1 0.98 falling to 0.94 as data quality worsened; durations differing by ~60 ms between signals), so absolute incomplete-blink ratios are not interchangeable across methods.

BOTTOM LINE. Registration of blinks by webcam EAR at ~0.75 of a personal baseline is supported and has a direct published precedent. Classification of those blinks as complete versus incomplete at 0.60 of baseline is, as far as this search can establish, an unvalidated novel measurement decision — running in the one condition (10 lux, dark-polarity display, downgaze reading) where the retrieved literature predicts the method fails worst.

### Implications raised for this protocol

**must fix**

- **The 0.60-of-baseline 'complete blink' threshold — the definition of the PRIMARY OUTCOME — has no published validation against any reference standard, in any population, at any frame rate. Every retrieved study that classifies complete versus incomplete blinks used near-IR iris/eyelid segmentation (Nousias 2022), keratograph deep learning (Zheng 2022), high-speed IR palpebral aperture height (Navascues-Cornago 2020) or a masked human grader (Wang 2025). The only EAR-based partial-blink work (Zhang & Echegoyen 2023) is n=10+10 and publishes neither its thresholds nor any sensitivity/specificity.**
  Run an internal validation sub-study BEFORE the main analysis and report it in the thesis as a distinct chapter. Recruit a stratified subsample of ~25-30 of the 130 (deliberately oversampling spectacle wearers and both illumination levels) and record each participant's reading epoch simultaneously with (a) the tablet front camera running the production pipeline and (b) a reference channel — a near-IR or high-frame-rate camera zoomed on the eye region. Have two graders blind to the EAR output classify every blink frame-by-frame as complete or incomplete, with a third senior grader adjudicating disagreements (the Nousias 2022 design). Report per-blink sensitivity, specificity and Cohen's kappa for the incomplete class, plus per-participant Bland-Altman limits of agreement for the incomplete-blink RATIO, which is the quantity actually analysed. Pre-specify a go/no-go: if the incomplete-class F1 falls materially below the ~0.81 that Nousias achieved with far better instrumentation, demote the incomplete-blink ratio from primary to secondary and promote a measure the instrument can support (e.g. blink rate or inter-blink interval).

- **Ambient illumination is the between-sitting IV and simultaneously the dominant determinant of measurement quality, so effect and error are aliased. Zhou (2022) tested MediaPipe blink detection directly: 95.9%/91.2% success under adequate light collapsing to 39.7%/48.8% under insufficient light without image enhancement. Chhimpa et al. (2024) independently report MediaPipe FaceMesh has 'a high level of sensitivity to low light conditions'. Worse, at 10 lux ambient the participant's face is lit mainly by the display itself — and display polarity is the within-sitting IV, so a negative-polarity screen (light text on dark) emits far less light onto the face than a positive-polarity screen. Face illumination therefore covaries with BOTH independent variables. This is inference from the cited mechanism, not a directly measured result, but it is first-order: any polarity or illumination effect on the incomplete-blink ratio at 10 lux is confounded with differential measurement error in the same direction.**
  Decouple face illumination from the IVs. Preferred fix: add a constant, task-invariant near-infrared illuminator directed at the face plus an IR-capable capture path, so ocular-region SNR is identical across all 10 conditions and both sittings; photometrically verify face-plane irradiance is constant across polarity and colour conditions and report it alongside the eye-position lux readings. If that is not possible: (a) log per-frame face-detection ratio and MediaPipe landmark confidence, and pre-register a minimum tracking yield per condition-epoch (e.g. ≥90% of frames with a detected face and confidence above a stated cut) with epochs below it excluded; (b) report tracking yield as an outcome broken down by polarity x colour x illumination, because a polarity effect on yield is itself evidence of the confound; (c) downgrade the 10 lux versus 150 lux comparison of the primary outcome from confirmatory to exploratory unless the IR fix is implemented. Do not apply a low-light enhancement filter (e.g. Zero-DCE) only to the dim sitting — that introduces a condition-specific processing difference; if used, apply it identically to all conditions.

- **Downgaze is a systematic confound of a baseline-normalised EAR ratio. Culemann et al. (2026) show eye openness at blink onset and closing amplitude both decrease with downward eye orientation, with blink duration rising 8-32% from upward to downward gaze; Nielsen et al. (2008) show lowering gaze angle 25 degrees significantly reduces the ocular surface area proxy. If the open-eye baseline is captured once at session start in primary gaze but blinks are measured during a four-page downgaze reading task, the denominator is systematically too large, both the 0.75 and 0.60 crossings are reached too easily, and the bias drifts as gaze moves down each page.**
  Three changes. (1) Fix and record the tablet position, viewing distance and vertical gaze angle identically across all 10 conditions and both sittings, and report the gaze angle in the methods — this is what makes Talens-Estarelles et al. (2021) non-comparable to Argilés et al. (2015). (2) Stop using a single session-start baseline: re-estimate the open-eye EAR reference WITHIN each condition from that condition's own reading frames (e.g. a rolling median or high percentile of non-blink EAR over the 180 s epoch), so the denominator tracks the actual gaze posture. Report both the session-baseline and within-condition-baseline versions of the primary outcome as a sensitivity analysis. (3) The protocol already derives gaze zone — include mean vertical gaze zone during the reading epoch as a covariate in the mixed model for the incomplete-blink ratio, and report whether it attenuates the polarity or colour effects.

- **The frame-rate gate is set on the wrong quantity and at the wrong level. The protocol gates duration-based measures below ~25 fps, but Navascues-Cornago et al. (2026) show duration and amplitude are the metrics that SURVIVE 25 fps (minimal bias, narrow LoA against a 500 fps reference), while velocities are the ones that fail (markedly underestimated below 250 fps). Meanwhile Zheng et al. (2022) tested incomplete-blink parameters specifically and found 30 fps materially better than 8 fps, recommending ≥30 fps. Against Nakamura et al.'s (2008) ~100 ms down-phase, 30 fps yields only about three frames in the closing phase and one to three frames at the EAR minimum — the exact frames on which the complete/incomplete call rests.**
  Set a hard floor of ≥30 fps for ALL blink metrics including the primary outcome, not ~25 fps and not only for duration (Zheng 2022). Log achieved median fps per condition-epoch and pre-register exclusion of any epoch below the floor. Delete every velocity-derived metric (closing speed, opening speed, amplitude-velocity ratio) from the protocol — they are unrecoverable below 250 fps (Navascues-Cornago 2026) and reporting them invites a reviewer challenge. Add, as a data-quality outcome, the number of frames per blink at or below the completeness threshold, and pre-register that blinks resolved by fewer than a stated minimum number of frames near the trough are flagged; also verify the tablet's capture is constant-rate rather than variable-frame-rate, since VFR capture silently breaks any frame-count-based duration or completeness estimate.

**should fix**

- **A 0.75-of-baseline registration threshold is at the permissive end of published practice (Ru 2026 uses 50% of baseline for detection; Dewi et al. 2022 show accuracy and AUC degrade as the EAR threshold rises), and a bare threshold crossing is not what the foundational method does — Soukupová & Čech classify a PATTERN of EAR values over a short temporal window. A permissive single-crossing rule will register squints, expression-driven lid movements and twitches as blinks, inflating the denominator of the incomplete-blink ratio and biasing the primary outcome upward in exactly the conditions that provoke squinting (dim ambient, low-contrast text colours).**
  Replace the bare crossing with an explicit event rule and pre-register it in full: a minimum number of consecutive frames below the registration threshold, a minimum EAR-trough prominence relative to the local open-eye level, a minimum and maximum event duration bounded by physiological blink kinematics (Nakamura 2008: total ~320 ms), and a refractory period between events. Then report the primary outcome at the planned 0.75/0.60 pair AND at a pre-specified sensitivity grid (e.g. registration 0.80 and 0.70; completeness 0.50 and 0.65) so a reviewer can see how much of any polarity or colour effect is threshold artefact. State plainly in the synopsis that 0.75 follows Ersoy et al. (2026) and that 0.60 is a novel, protocol-specific decision being validated internally.

- **MediaPipe FaceMesh EAR has no clinical validation, and EAR itself is geometrically unstable. Wolter et al. (2025) show EAR is viewpoint-dependent because it uses 2D landmark distances that distort under head rotation, with substantially higher variance across camera sweeps than a 3D eyelid-angle metric computed from the same MediaPipe mesh. The protocol runs 10 conditions plus washouts per sitting with no chin rest mentioned, so head pose will drift within and between conditions.**
  Two additions. (1) The protocol already derives head pose — use it: pre-register exclusion or down-weighting of frames beyond a stated yaw/pitch bound, and include mean head pose per condition as a covariate; report head-pose distributions per condition to show they do not differ systematically by polarity or colour. (2) Compute, in parallel from the same MediaPipe 3D mesh, a viewpoint-robust alternative — an eyelid-angle-style 3D metric, or an EAR normalised by inter-canthal distance with head-pose correction — and pre-register agreement between the two as a headline sensitivity analysis. Concordance between two geometrically different metrics from the same landmarks is the cheapest available evidence that the incomplete-blink finding is not a projection artefact. Also state explicitly in the synopsis that no retrieved study validates MediaPipe FaceMesh EAR against a high-speed-video, EOG, IR eye-tracking or expert-graded reference standard for blink completeness.

- **Spectacles are an unquantified failure mode that interacts with the IV. Chhimpa et al. (2024) report MediaPipe FaceMesh shows 'a moderate reaction to glasses'; the peer reviews of Dewi et al. (2022) note blink-detection accuracy is reduced by shadows cast by glasses and poor lighting; Nousias et al. (2022) tolerated spectacle glare only by using near-IR and segmentation. Critically, the display is the dominant reflection source in the lenses at 10 lux, and display polarity changes that reflection — so spectacle artefact is not a nuisance factor but one that covaries with the within-sitting IV.**
  Record at the profile visit: spectacle wear (and whether worn during the task), lens type, anti-reflective coating, and frame rim position relative to the eye. Pre-register spectacle wear as a covariate and a planned subgroup analysis of the primary outcome. In the validation sub-study, deliberately stratify recruitment so spectacle wearers are represented and report classification accuracy separately for them. Add a pre-flight check that captures a still frame per polarity and flags any specular reflection overlapping the eye region; if the tablet position can be tilted a few degrees to move the screen reflection off the palpebral aperture without changing gaze angle, do so and record the geometry.

- **A single fractional completeness threshold assumes the EAR-to-lid-gap mapping is the same across participants at the CLOSED end, which baseline normalisation does not deliver. Gao et al. (2024) found significant Caucasian-Chinese differences in palpebral fissure height, iris diameter, palpebral fissure index, lateral canthal angle and canthal tilt, with less prominent double-eyelid folds in the Chinese group. EAR is a height-to-width ratio, so palpebral fissure index sets its dynamic range: normalising by each person's open-eye EAR rescales the open end, but two participants at 0.60 x baseline can still have materially different actual lid gaps. An Assam university cohort will span a wide range of eyelid morphologies including epicanthic folds.**
  Record objective periocular measures at the profile visit — palpebral fissure height and width (hence palpebral fissure index), and a simple lid-crease / epicanthal-fold classification — on every participant, not just a subsample. In the validation sub-study, test whether complete/incomplete classification error correlates with palpebral fissure index and epicanthal-fold presence. If it does, report the primary outcome with palpebral fissure index as a covariate, and consider deriving each participant's completeness threshold from their own closed-eye calibration (a brief instructed full-closure sequence during setup, giving a per-participant EAR floor) rather than from a fixed 0.60 fraction of the open-eye baseline — this anchors the ratio at both ends instead of only one.

- **The 60 s / 120 s neutral-grey washout has no evidentiary basis in anything retrieved, and there is a specific reason to doubt it for THIS outcome. Nielsen et al. (2008) found blink frequency during an active VDU task was 69% lower than passive, and that this suppression 'was succeeded by a burst with high BF after cessation of the active task, indicating a compensatory blinking process'. If blink behaviour has not returned to baseline when the next condition begins, carry-over contaminates every condition after the first, and a Williams square balances but does not remove a carry-over that differs by condition.**
  Do not assert a washout duration you cannot defend. In a pilot (or in the first cohort, analysed before unblinding the main analysis), record blink metrics continuously THROUGH the grey adaptation field and plot the recovery time-course of blink rate and incomplete-blink ratio; set the washout to the time at which both return to within a pre-specified band of the pre-task baseline in the large majority of participants, and report that empirical basis in the methods rather than citing the 60/120 s figures as given. Regardless of the duration chosen, log blink metrics during every washout in the main study so carry-over can be TESTED — include period and first-order carry-over terms in the mixed model, and report the pre-condition washout blink rate as a covariate. Note that no retrieved study bears on the 60 s versus 120 s polarity-switch distinction specifically; that asymmetry is currently an untested assumption.

**document only**

- **Cross-study comparability of the incomplete-blink ratio. Nyström et al. (2024) and Culemann et al. (2023) both show blink metrics differ systematically with device, algorithm and blink definition (durations differing by ~60 ms between signals; F1 falling 0.98 to 0.94 with data quality). Wang et al. (2025) established a ≥40% incomplete-blink Youden threshold for dry eye — but by masked human grading, and Chen et al. (2026) report ~60% IBR by front camera. These numbers are not interchangeable with an EAR-derived ratio.**
  Report the full instrument specification in the methods and the supplement: the exact MediaPipe landmark indices used for the EAR numerator and denominator, the EAR formula, the smoothing filter and its parameters, both thresholds and how the baseline was estimated, the minimum blink duration and refractory period, the achieved frame rate distribution, and the tracking yield. Publish the analysis code. In the discussion, explicitly decline to compare absolute incomplete-blink percentages against LipiView, keratograph or masked-observer literature (including Wang et al.'s 40% threshold), and frame all findings as within-study, within-instrument contrasts across the polarity, colour and illumination conditions — which is what the crossover design licenses and all that it licenses.

## Text colour, at matched luminance contrast, has a measurable effect on visual fatigue and reading performance beyond the effect of contrast itself.

**Verdict: `partially_supported`** (unverified evidence)

### What the searches reported

The claim splits cleanly into two halves that the literature answers differently, and the protocol currently treats them as one.

READING PERFORMANCE: the evidence is against the claim, and the studies that speak to it most directly are the ones that actually held luminance contrast fixed. Pastoor (1990, Human Factors) fixed luminance contrast at two pre-optimised levels (10:1 and 1:6.5), had 32 observers rate ~800 colour combinations and 18 observers perform reading and search timing on 18 combinations, and found "no evidence for an influence of luminance polarity or chromaticity on performance"; saturation, not hue, drove subjective ratings. Buchner and Baumgartner (2007, Ergonomics) ran a series of proofreading experiments and found the positive-polarity advantage was independent of chromaticity (black/white vs blue/yellow), and that colour contrast (red on green) could not compensate for absent luminance contrast; their physiological strain measures (heart rate, HRV, breathing rate, skin conductance) and self-reported eyestrain, headache and fatigue did not vary with any independent variable. Legge, Parish, Luebker and Wurm (1990, JOSA A) is the mechanistic anchor: reading-speed-versus-contrast curves have the same shape for colour contrast and luminance contrast and superimpose when contrast is expressed in multiples of its own threshold, with no additive interaction — performance is set by whichever form of contrast yields the higher rate. That is precisely a "colour acts like contrast, not as an extra factor" result. Colour contrast conferred no advantage for their low-vision observers at 6-degree characters.

VISUAL FATIGUE / COMFORT: here there is genuine, recent support, but for chroma and colour difference rather than for hue. Li, Liu, Zhu and Luo (2024, Vision Research; 230 stimuli, 20 observers) found the effect of text HUE on visual comfort was NOT significant, that comfort fell as text colour approached the background colour, and that a model including both lightness and chroma outperformed a lightness-only model — chroma adds predictive variance beyond lightness contrast. Penacchio, Haigh, Ross, Ferguson and Wilkins (2021, Frontiers in Neuroscience) showed that mean local chromaticity difference in an image predicts visual discomfort and "accounts for part of the variance left unexplained by variations in luminance". Wilkins (2025, Vision Research) reviews the mechanism — cortical hyperexcitability driven by unnatural spatial and chromatic image statistics — and stresses large individual differences in susceptibility. Allen, Hussain, Usherwood and Wilkins (2010, IOVS) is the sharpest moderator result: an individually chosen background chromaticity reduced accommodative lag by 0.16 D and improved reading speed by 10%, but ONLY in the 11 pattern-related-visual-stress participants; in matched symptom-free controls the coloured background slightly INCREASED lag.

CHROMATIC ABERRATION / BLUE TEXT: LCA produces a real, measurable dioptric offset but the evidence does not establish it as a fatigue driver in young normals. Jaskulski et al. (2016, OPO) measured a 0.45 +/- 0.09 D accommodative lead for blue and a 0.49 +/- 0.10 D lag for red relative to white — roughly a 0.9 D chromatic spread — but found no effect on depth of field and no dependence on accommodative demand. Fernandez-Alonso, Finch, Love and Read (2024, Journal of Vision) found observers accommodate accurately under narrowband LED illuminants and compensate for LCA, with no increase in steady-state variability versus broadband; at equal luminance, acuity was worst under blue narrowband light, attributed to LCA changing fastest at short wavelengths so an LED's finite bandwidth spans a larger dioptric range. Atchison, Strang and Stark (2004, OVS) found multichromatic and near-isoluminant targets did not significantly increase accommodative variability, but with n=5 the confidence intervals could not rule out moderate-to-large effects.

CONTRAST IS NOT MONOTONIC IN ITS EFFECT. Legge, Rubin and Luebker (1987, Vision Research) found reading was "very tolerant to contrast reduction — for 1-degree letters, reading rate decreased by less than a factor of two for a tenfold reduction in contrast", with steep decline only near threshold. Rubin and Legge (1989) formalised this as a "critical contrast" (contrast at which reading rate halves), which was on average 3.9x higher in low-vision than normal observers and correlated r = 0.87 with letter contrast sensitivity. So the function is a broad plateau with a knee, not a straight line — a single sub-AA point cannot be fitted as a linear covariate.

THE MOST-CITED SUPPORT FOR THIS PROTOCOL'S HYPOTHESIS IS THE WEAKEST STUDY IN THE SET. Fan, Xie, Dong and Wang (2024, Sensors) reports the red-worst / yellow-best ordering that the protocol appears to lean on. Reading its full text (PMC11175232): text colour was a BETWEEN-subjects factor with 50 participants in 5 groups of 10; colours were equated on HSB brightness and saturation (B=62%, S=70%), which is not photometric luminance, so hue is confounded with luminance and Michelson contrast against the black background; illumination was 0, 5 and 10 lux, entirely below this protocol's 150 lux arm; and the reported test for the colour main effect is F(4,145) with eta-squared = 0.914 — degrees of freedom consistent with treating each participant's three sessions as independent observations, which inflates a between-subjects effect. It should be cited as motivation, not as an effect size to power from.

AT REVIEW LEVEL, DISPLAY COLOUR IS SIMPLY NOT YET EVIDENCED. Mataftsi et al. (2023, Preventive Medicine), a systematic review of digital eye strain in pre-presbyopic users restricted to studies using validated questionnaires (10 studies, 2365 participants, GRADE-assessed), identified only screen duration (>4-5 h/day) and poor ergonomic parameters as aggravating factors, and found no evidence supporting blue-blocking filters. Display text colour does not appear among the evidenced factors at all.

NET: at matched luminance contrast, hue has no established effect on reading performance and the best-designed studies report a null; chroma/colour-difference does have a small, replicated effect on subjective visual comfort; and any hue effect on discomfort appears to be individually specific rather than population-general. The claim as written is too strong for performance and roughly right for comfort provided "colour" is read as chroma and colour difference rather than hue.

Note on recency: 8 of 17 citations are 2021 or later. The foundational psychophysics (Legge 1987/1990, Pastoor 1990) is old because it has not been superseded — no retrieved recent study repeats those luminance-matched manipulations with modern displays, which is exactly the gap this protocol could fill.

### Implications raised for this protocol

**must fix**

- **Text colour and luminance contrast are confounded. The design lists 5 colours per polarity with WCAG contrast merely 'recorded as a covariate', and one cell at 2.39:1. If the 10 cells have 10 different luminance contrasts, the colour main effect is uninterpretable and the claim cannot be tested at all - only the trivially true statement that contrast matters.**
  Before data collection, measure L_text and L_background in cd/m2 for all 10 text/background pairs with a spot photometer or spectroradiometer at the eye position, under BOTH ambient levels (10 and 150 lux), with the actual tablet at its locked brightness. Then re-specify the palette so that within each polarity all 5 colours are equated on measured Weber contrast to within +/-5% against the common background. Do NOT equate on HSB brightness, RGB code values, or sRGB relative luminance - Fan et al. (2024) equated on HSB B=62%/S=70% and thereby produced a 'colour effect' that is inseparable from luminance. If a contrast gradient is also wanted, obtain it by adding a separate contrast factor with its own levels, not by letting contrast ride along with hue.

(1) READING PERFORMANCE — well supported, and the best-replicated part of the claim. The positive-polarity advantage is robust for legibility and proofreading in young adults, and the Düsseldorf group established both its boundary conditions and its mechanism. Buchner & Baumgartner (Ergonomics 2007) found better proofreading with positive polarity across a series of experiments, and showed it was independent of ambient lighting (darkness vs typical office illumination) and of chromaticity (black/white vs blue/yellow), and that colour contrast (red on green) could not substitute for luminance contrast. Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner (Human Factors 2014) showed the advantage grows linearly as character size shrinks across 8/10/12/14 pt (0.22–0.34 deg vertical visual angle) — i.e. it is a detail-perception effect. Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner (Ergonomics 2014) measured pupil size directly and found smaller pupils AND better proofreading with positive polarity, supporting the display-luminance hypothesis: brighter positive-polarity screens constrict the pupil, sharpening the retinal image. This matters enormously for the present protocol: on the dominant mechanistic account, "polarity" is a proxy for mean display luminance, not an independent factor.

(2) VISUAL FATIGUE — contested, and the classic literature actually argues AGAINST the fatigue half of the claim. Buchner & Baumgartner's own physiological and subjective measures — breathing rate, heart rate, HRV, skin conductance, self-reported mood, fatigue, arousal, eyestrain, headache, muscle strain and back pain — did NOT vary with polarity, ambient light or chromaticity. Their explicit interpretation was that participants worked equally hard in all conditions; the polarity effect was on performance, not on strain. Recent work is genuinely split. Supporting fatigue effects: Lin, Ji & Lin (Ergonomics 2024, N=36 for the polarity contrast) found a positive-polarity advantage in both visual fatigue and discomfort on a desktop head-up display under 300 lx, and derived an optimum display luminance of 153 cd/m² from an inverted-U model. Sethi & Ziat (Ergonomics 2023) found higher cognitive load with negative polarity via increased search time and pupil diameter, and higher mental-effort scores — but conditionally: for older adults in a BRIGHT environment and for younger adults in a DIM environment; younger adults nonetheless preferred negative polarity for aesthetic reasons. Against: Sengsoon & Intaruk (Int J Environ Res Public Health 2025), a randomised crossover in 30 female tablet users, found NO significant difference in visual fatigue between light and dark mode after 1 h; critical flicker frequency and dry-eye symptoms differed significantly and in DARK MODE'S FAVOUR, and both modes worsened all three outcomes from baseline. Their discussion also reports a null dark-vs-light finding from Pedersen et al. (2020) with a preference for dark mode, though I could not retrieve that paper independently and do not cite it. Fan, Xie, Dong & Wang (Sensors 2024) — the study closest to the present protocol's design — held polarity constant at negative and varied five text colours × 0/5/10 lux; text colour dominated everything (partial eta-squared 0.914 for pupil accommodation, 0.861 for blink rate, 0.778 for subjective fatigue), ambient illumination had far smaller effects (0.202 and 0.141) and NO significant effect on the subjective visual-fatigue index or on cognitive performance. Red text was consistently worst and yellow best. Important methodological caveat: their reported degrees of freedom (F(4,145), F(2,147), F(8,141)) indicate the 150 trials were treated as independent even though illumination was within-subject and colour between-subject, so those eta-squared values are almost certainly inflated and should not be used naively to anchor power.

(3) OCULAR BEHAVIOUR / OCULAR-SURFACE MEASURES — this is where the strongest, cleanest and most recent polarity evidence lives, and it points in the OPPOSITE valence to the performance literature. Aleman, Wang & Schaeffel (Scientific Reports 2018) showed that black text on white heavily overstimulates retinal OFF pathways and thinned the choroid by ~16 µm in one hour, whereas white text on black overstimulated ON pathways and thickened it by ~10 µm. Hoseini-Yazdi, Read, Alonso-Caneiro & Collins (IOVS 2021, n=20) is the decisive luminance-controlled test: mean luminance (35 cd/m²), Michelson contrast (87%) and letter size (~11.8 arcmin) were MATCHED across polarities, and OFF-pathway overstimulation (dark text on bright) still produced significantly greater subfoveal choroidal thinning (−9 ± 1 µm) than ON-pathway (−4 ± 1 µm, p = 0.002) or an unbiased control (−2 ± 1 µm, p < 0.001), with an additive −7 µm contribution from 5 D accommodation. That is a polarity effect on ocular physiology that cannot be explained away as a luminance artefact — which is exactly what the Piepenbrock account would predict for performance but not here. Swiatczak & Schaeffel (Transl Vis Sci Technol 2022) added two moderators the present protocol should care about: refractive status (myopes, −3.5 ± 1.9 D, n=11, still showed −10.2 ± 9.5 µm axial shortening with inverted contrast, as emmetropes do) and letter size (with large text, 34.39 arcmin, standard contrast +1.7 ± 9.0 µm vs inverted −9.7 ± 8.9 µm, p = 0.0017; with small text, 13.75 arcmin, no significant difference, p = 0.09). Ingrassia, Swiatczak & Schaeffel (Vision Research 2024) replicated the inverted-contrast shortening and showed it can be abolished by an interacting chromatic manipulation. Swiatczak's 2025 award review (Ophthalmic Physiol Opt) confirms the effect in both emmetropic and myopic eyes.

(4) THE PROTOCOL'S PRIMARY OUTCOME HAS NO PRIOR EVIDENCE BASE AT ALL. I ran five distinct query formulations for polarity crossed with blinking and with blink completeness. The only paper returned that touches both is Fan et al. 2024, and it reports blink RATE within negative polarity only — never completeness, and never a polarity contrast. Conversely, the incomplete-blink literature (Portello, Rosenfield & Chu, Optom Vis Sci 2013; Talens-Estarelles et al., Clin Exp Optom 2022 and Cont Lens Anterior Eye 2022) never manipulates polarity. So the claim as stated is supported for reading performance, contested for visual fatigue, and supported for ocular behaviour ONLY in the choroidal/axial-length sense — while the specific ocular-behaviour endpoint this protocol has nominated as primary (incomplete-blink ratio) has, on the evidence retrievable in this session, never been tested against display polarity by anyone. That is a defensible novelty claim for the thesis, but it means the study is powered on nothing and must be framed as hypothesis-generating for that endpoint.

(5) INTERACTION WITH AMBIENT ILLUMINATION — contested, and the protocol's specific levels are poorly supported. Buchner & Baumgartner explicitly found NO polarity × ambient-lighting interaction across darkness vs office illumination — the strongest single piece of evidence against the protocol's between-sitting factor mattering. Sethi & Ziat found an interaction, but a three-way one (age × polarity × ambient), which a 20–24-year-old-only sample cannot detect. Lin et al. tested polarity at a single illuminance (300 lx). Fan et al. found colour × illumination interaction on blink rate (partial eta-squared 0.218) but none on subjective fatigue, and their entire illumination range (0–10 lux) sits at or below the protocol's "dim" level.

(6) AGE AND REFRACTIVE STATUS — Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund & Buchner (Ergonomics 2013) tested visual acuity and proofreading in younger and older adults and found the positive-polarity advantage in BOTH, concluding positive polarity should be recommended regardless of age; light scatter in the senescent lens did not reverse it. Sethi & Ziat's flip of the load penalty across age × ambient is the counterweight. On refractive status, Swiatczak & Schaeffel is the only direct evidence and shows the choroidal polarity response survives in myopia; Sengsoon & Intaruk excluded all ametropes (and all men), which sharply limits generalisability of their null.

BOTTOM LINE: the sentence as written is too strong on "visual fatigue" and too vague on "ocular behaviour". A defensible restatement: "Display polarity has a well-replicated effect on reading/legibility performance, attributable substantially to display luminance and pupil size; its effect on subjective visual fatigue is inconsistent across studies and was explicitly null in the largest classical series; and it has a robust, luminance-independent effect on choroidal thickness and axial length that runs opposite in valence to the performance advantage. Its effect on blink completeness is untested."

### Implications raised for this protocol

**must fix**

- **The stated primary outcome — incomplete-blink ratio during reading — has no prior polarity literature whatsoever. Five distinct query formulations crossing polarity with blinking/blink-completeness returned exactly one polarity-adjacent blink paper (Fan et al. 2024), which held polarity constant at negative and reported blink RATE, never completeness. The incomplete-blink literature (Portello 2013; Talens-Estarelles 2022a, 2022b) never manipulates polarity. The synopsis therefore cannot claim its primary hypothesis is grounded in prior effect estimates.**
  Split the hypotheses explicitly. Designate reading/legibility performance and subjective comfort as CONFIRMATORY hypotheses (grounded in Buchner & Baumgartner 2007 and the Piepenbrock series) and designate incomplete-blink ratio as an EXPLORATORY primary endpoint, stating in the synopsis that no prior study has tested display polarity against blink completeness. Power the study on the confirmatory endpoints and report the blink endpoint with a pre-registered estimation (effect size + CI) framing rather than a significance test framing.

- **Display luminance is the dominant mechanistic explanation for the classic polarity advantage (Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner, Ergonomics 2014: smaller pupils AND better proofreading with positive polarity). If mean screen luminance is not matched or measured across the 10 polarity x colour cells, 'polarity' in this study is uninterpretable — it is a luminance manipulation wearing a polarity label. Czerniak et al. 2020 further show ocular indices track constant screen illumination independently of task load.**
  Extend the existing display-photometry step to record, per condition cell (2 polarities x 5 colours), the mean screen luminance in cd/m2 over a representative page of the actual stimulus text, plus the Michelson luminance contrast between glyph and background. Either (a) match mean luminance across polarities as Hoseini-Yazdi et al. did (35 cd/m2) and Swiatczak & Schaeffel did (86 +/- 7 cd/m2) — the only way to claim a luminance-independent polarity effect — or (b) if matching is refused for ecological validity, pre-register per-cell mean luminance as a continuous covariate in every model and report polarity effects both adjusted and unadjusted.

- **The EAR-threshold definition of the primary outcome (blink registered at <=0.75 of the participant's open-eye baseline, complete at <=0.60) is an unvalidated heuristic. de la Cruz et al. (IEEE TNNLS 2024) treat blink-completeness detection as a distinct classification task needing a CNN plus bidirectional recurrent model and a Siamese architecture specifically to handle severe class imbalance — i.e. the field does not regard two fixed EAR cut-points as sufficient. A primary outcome defined by an unvalidated threshold pair will be the first thing an examiner attacks.**
  Add a measurement-validation sub-study before or alongside the main data collection: in a subsample (e.g. 15-20 participants), record synchronised high-frame-rate video and have two masked graders classify each blink as complete/incomplete, then report sensitivity, specificity and Cohen's kappa of the 0.75/0.60 EAR rule against that reference, plus a threshold-sensitivity analysis (re-running the primary analysis at, say, 0.70/0.55 and 0.80/0.65). Report the achieved frame rate distribution per participant and per condition, since the 25 fps gate means missingness on duration-based measures will be non-random across devices.

- **The 180 s per-condition exposure is an order of magnitude shorter than every study that has detected a polarity effect on an ocular or fatigue endpoint. Aleman et al. used 1 hour; Hoseini-Yazdi et al., Swiatczak & Schaeffel and Ingrassia et al. used 30 minutes; Lin et al. used a full ergonomic session under fixed lighting; Sengsoon & Intaruk used 1 hour per mode; Portello measured incomplete blinks over a continuous 15-minute task; Talens-Estarelles et al. moved ocular-surface signs with 30 minutes. Nothing in the retrieved literature shows a polarity effect at 3 minutes.**
  Either lengthen per-condition reading to at least 10-15 minutes (which would force a reduction in the number of conditions per sitting, e.g. by moving text colour to a between-subjects or between-sitting factor), or explicitly reframe the study as testing IMMEDIATE-onset differences and state in the synopsis that it is not designed to detect the cumulative ocular-surface and choroidal effects that the 30-60 minute literature reports. In the second case, drop any per-condition inference from the CVS-Q (which is a session-level instrument over ~30 minutes of total reading) and report CVS-Q change only as a session-level, illumination-level outcome.

- **The five text colours are the largest effect in the closest analogue study — Fan et al. 2024 report partial eta-squared 0.914 (pupil accommodation), 0.861 (blink rate) and 0.778 (subjective fatigue) for text colour, dwarfing ambient illumination — but that effect is uninterpretable unless colours are equated for luminance contrast. Fan et al. held HSB brightness at 62% and saturation at 70% across blue/red/yellow/green and used white at B=95% with Michelson contrast 0.969 against black. Without equating, a 'text colour' effect in this protocol is a contrast effect.**
  Specify each of the five text colours by exact sRGB and by measured luminance, and report the Michelson luminance contrast and WCAG contrast ratio for every colour x polarity cell in the methods table. Either equate luminance contrast across colours within each polarity, or add contrast ratio as a covariate. Also pre-register the direction predicted by Fan et al. under negative polarity (yellow best, red worst) so the colour analysis is confirmatory rather than a fishing expedition across 5 levels x 2 polarities x 2 illuminances.

- **The primary outcome is a bounded proportion with a between-subject SD approximately equal to its mean (Portello et al. 2013: mean 16.1%, SD 15.7%, range 0.9-56.5%, n=21 over 15 min). Analysing it as a Gaussian percentage will be badly mis-specified — floor effects, right skew, and heteroscedasticity across participants — and a power calculation based on a normal-theory paired t-test will be wrong.**
  Pre-register a binomial or beta-binomial GLMM on the raw counts (incomplete blinks out of total registered blinks per condition) with random intercepts for participant and, if estimable, random slopes for polarity, rather than a t-test or ANOVA on a percentage. Anchor the power simulation on Portello's dispersion (mean 16.1%, SD 15.7%) and simulate rather than use a closed-form formula; explicitly state that the within-participant ICC across the 10 conditions in a sitting is unknown and will be estimated, with power reported across a plausible ICC range (e.g. 0.3-0.7). Also note that Portello found forcing blink rate up did not change symptoms, so blink rate and blink completeness should not be treated as interchangeable indices of the same construct.

- **The synopsis's framing ('polarity affects visual fatigue') overstates the evidence and omits the direct contradictions. Buchner & Baumgartner found NO polarity effect on self-reported fatigue, eyestrain, headache or on breathing rate, heart rate, HRV or skin conductance. Sengsoon & Intaruk's randomised crossover found no visual-fatigue difference between modes and found dark mode BETTER on critical flicker frequency and dry-eye symptoms. Meanwhile the meta-analysis of asthenopia risk factors (Song et al.) lists no polarity or display-mode variable among pooled risk or protective factors.**
  Rewrite the claim in the synopsis as three separable statements with their true evidentiary weight: (i) polarity has a well-replicated effect on legibility/proofreading performance, mediated substantially by display luminance and pupil size; (ii) its effect on subjective visual fatigue is inconsistent, with null and reversed findings in the two best-controlled sources; (iii) it has a luminance-independent effect on choroidal thickness and axial length that runs OPPOSITE in valence to the performance advantage (positive polarity reads better but thins the choroid). Cite the nulls (Buchner & Baumgartner 2007; Sengsoon & Intaruk 2025) in the literature review, not only the supportive work — a synopsis that cites only confirmatory evidence on a contested question is the easiest thing for an examiner to flag.

- **Only PubMed was reachable in this session; the HCI venues that publish much of the modern dark-mode work (ACM CHI/ETRA/SUI, IEEE VR) were unreachable, and specific papers surfaced by WebSearch could not be verified and so are not cited here. The evidence base summarised above is therefore biased toward ophthalmology and Ergonomics-journal sources.**
  Before submitting the synopsis, re-run the literature search in Scopus or Web of Science with the term set used here plus 'contrast polarity', 'light-on-dark', 'inverted contrast' and 'theme', and specifically retrieve and assess: the Erickson/Kim/Bruder/Welch dark-mode HMD papers (2019 SUI and 2020 IEEE VR), Pedersen et al. 2020 on dark vs light mode, and the 2025 ETRA eye-tracking study on dark and light themes. Do not cite any of these on the strength of a secondary description; obtain the primary source.

**should fix**

- **The washout durations (60 s neutral grey, 120 s at a polarity switch) are not supported by any retrieved evidence, and the closest analogues used far longer. Sengsoon & Intaruk allowed >=30 minutes between modes AND required symptom scores to return to baseline before proceeding. Talens-Estarelles et al. (2022, 20-20-20 trial) found short breaks moved symptoms but produced no change in any objective ocular-surface or blink measure, so a 60 s field cannot be assumed to reset blink behaviour.**
  Replace the fixed-duration washout with a criterion-based one: set the grey field to the mean luminance of the two polarities (not an arbitrary grey), and require the participant's live EAR open-eye baseline and blink rate to return within a pre-specified band of their own session baseline (e.g. within 1 SD of the calibration window) before the next condition starts, with a hard floor of 60 s and a cap of 180 s. Independently, exploit the Williams square: pre-register a model containing period and first-order carryover terms, and report the estimated polarity carryover coefficient with its CI as evidence about washout adequacy rather than asserting it.

- **Comprehension accuracy (3 MCQs: gist, inference, detail) is a near-useless dependent variable for detecting a polarity effect, and it is not the outcome the literature's replicated effect lives in. Every replication of the positive-polarity advantage (Buchner & Baumgartner 2007; Piepenbrock 2013; Piepenbrock 2014 x2) used PROOFREADING error detection or visual acuity, not comprehension. A 0-3 score per condition has almost no variance and a ceiling.**
  Add a proofreading/legibility measure to each condition as a positive control — e.g. embed a fixed number of typographical errors in the 600-word passage and score hits and false alarms, matching the Buchner/Piepenbrock paradigm. This gives the study a chance of replicating the one polarity effect that is actually well established, which is what a reviewer will look for before believing any novel blink finding. If the passage cannot carry embedded errors, at minimum increase the MCQ count substantially or report reading speed as the primary performance endpoint and demote comprehension to a manipulation check that participants engaged with the text.

- **The polarity x ambient-illumination interaction — the reason for the two-sitting design — has the weakest prior support of anything in the protocol. Buchner & Baumgartner (2007) explicitly found NO polarity x ambient-lighting interaction across darkness vs typical office illumination. Sethi & Ziat (2023) found one, but as a three-way interaction with AGE, which a 20-24-year-old sample cannot detect. Fan et al. (2024) found ambient illumination had no significant effect on subjective fatigue at all across 0/5/10 lux, and the protocol's 'dim' level of 10 lux sits at the very top of that null range.**
  State the Buchner & Baumgartner null explicitly in the synopsis rather than citing only supportive work, and power the study for polarity and colour MAIN effects, treating the polarity x illumination interaction as secondary with a pre-registered smallest effect size of interest. Consider raising the contrast of the illumination manipulation (e.g. 10 lux vs 500 lux rather than 150 lux) so that the two sittings actually straddle the range over which Sethi & Ziat and Lin et al. observed effects; 150 lux is below the 300 lx office level at which Lin et al. derived their 153 cd/m2 optimum. Log lux at eye position AND screen luminance at the same three timepoints, and analyse the screen-to-ambient luminance RATIO, which is the variable Lin et al. showed to matter, not lux alone.

- **Refractive status is a demonstrated moderator of polarity effects on ocular measures (Swiatczak & Schaeffel 2022: myopes -3.5 +/- 1.9 D still showed -10.2 +/- 9.5 um axial shortening with inverted contrast; Swiatczak 2025 confirms the response in both emmetropes and myopes but weaker myopic responses to defocus). The protocol's session-level workup includes Ishihara colour screening and photometry but does not state that refraction is captured.**
  Add objective autorefraction (or at minimum habitual spectacle Rx and a subjective refraction) plus habitual correction worn during the task to the participant profile, and pre-register spherical equivalent as a moderator of the polarity effect. Also record whether contact lenses are worn, since lens wear independently affects blink completeness and ocular-surface endpoints. In a 130-student Indian university sample, myopia prevalence will be substantial and unbalanced across the Williams-square orders unless stratified.

**consider**

- **The strongest and most novel recent polarity finding — the ON/OFF-pathway effect on choroidal thickness and axial length (Aleman 2018; Hoseini-Yazdi 2021; Swiatczak & Schaeffel 2022; Ingrassia 2024; Swiatczak 2025) — is entirely absent from the protocol's outcome set, even though it is an optometry thesis with a display-polarity factor and the effect is letter-size gated in a way this protocol could test.**
  If OCT or optical biometry (e.g. low-coherence interferometry) is available at the institution, add a pre/post axial-length or subfoveal choroidal-thickness measurement at the SITTING level (not per condition — the effect needs 30-60 min of exposure). Even a single pre/post pair per sitting would let the thesis speak to the myopia-control literature and would be its most citable contribution. If not available, add an explicit paragraph to the discussion/limitations acknowledging this literature and stating that the study cannot address whether the positive-polarity performance advantage comes at an ocular-growth cost. Report letter size in arcmin (not points) either way, since Swiatczak & Schaeffel showed the polarity effect appears at 34.39 arcmin and vanishes at 13.75 arcmin.

## The CVS-Q is a validated instrument for this population and this design, and its change from baseline over a single ~90-minute session is a meaningful secondary outcome.

**Verdict: `partially_supported`** (unverified evidence)

### What the searches reported

Attribution: all bibliographic records below were retrieved from PubMed via the PubMed MCP server (search_articles / get_article_metadata / get_full_text_article / find_related_articles), supplemented by WebSearch and WebFetch. DOI links are given for every citation.

THE INSTRUMENT IS REAL AND WELL BUILT — the first half of the claim is largely, but not entirely, supported. The CVS-Q was developed and Rasch-validated by Seguí, Cabrero-García, Crespo, Verdú and Ronda in the Journal of Clinical Epidemiology (2015) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2015.01.015). The scoring rule, confirmed verbatim from two independent open-access validations, is: 16 ocular/visual symptoms; frequency scored 0 = never, 1 = occasionally, 2 = often/always; intensity scored 1 = moderate, 2 = intense; severity = frequency × intensity, then RECODED 0 = 0; 1 or 2 = 1; 4 = 2; severities summed, so the range is 0–32. The Colombian validation adds an implementation detail your scoring code must handle explicitly: "an intensity score of '0: none' was assigned when the frequency was reported as 'never,' as this category is not included in the original questionnaire" [DOI](https://doi.org/10.15649/cuidarte.3963). The original symptomatic cut-off is ≥6; sensitivity and specificity exceed 70% (reported as 75.0% / 70.2%, AUC 0.826); test–retest ICC = 0.802 (95% CI 0.673–0.884) with κ = 0.612 for the CVS classification. Cut-offs are VERSION-SPECIFIC: the Italian (≥7, sens 80.0%, spec 83.1%, AUC 0.874, ICC 0.725) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19084506) and Portuguese (≥7, α 0.793, ICC 0.847, sens 78.5%, spec 70.7%, AUC 0.832) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.5935/0004-2749.2022-0256) versions moved the threshold. The Chinese CVS-Q CN is a translation and cross-cultural adaptation ONLY — that paper reports comprehensibility and feasibility, not reliability or validity [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12886-023-03031-y).

BUT THE VALIDATED POPULATIONS ARE WORKERS, NOT STUDENTS. Every adult validation was conducted in video-display-terminal / digital-device WORKERS: Italian n = 241, mean age 45.5 ± 11.0 y; Portuguese n = 280 workers; Colombian n = 300 university employees. The only youth version, CVS-Q teen, is validated for ages 12–17 (n = 251, cut-off ≥6, sens 85.2%, spec 76.5%, AUC 0.879, ICC 0.77, κ 0.49) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-70821-9), and carries a Publisher Correction [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-81622-5). A 20–24-year-old university-student sample therefore sits in a validation gap between the two bands. Using the adult version is defensible (they are adults) but the synopsis must not assert validation "for this population."

THE RECALL FRAME IS THE REAL PROBLEM — and it argues against the second half of the claim. The CVS-Q's criterion validity is anchored, in the original and in every derivative, to the criterion "occurrence of at least one symptom two or three times a week." Its frequency anchors are never / occasionally / often-or-always. That is unambiguously a habitual, period-recall framing, not a present-state framing. Worse for the protocol: test–retest reliability was established over 7–15 day intervals by demonstrating the ABSENCE of change (Italian: "No differences were observed between the mean scores obtained before and after (p = 0.440)"; Colombian: stability 0.86 at a mean 9-day interval; CVS-Q teen: no difference between administrations, p = 0.491). In other words, the instrument was deliberately engineered to be STABLE over exactly the timescale you propose to move it. A participant answering the standard stem honestly twice, 90 minutes apart, should score identically; any difference is response drift, not visual fatigue.

HOWEVER — AND THIS IS THE EVIDENCE THAT RESCUES PART OF THE CLAIM — it empirically does move, in exactly your population and exactly your session length. Song, Li, Zhang, Tian, Li, Lai, He and Chen (JMIR Human Factors, 2026) ran 38 participants aged 19–31 (mean 24.89, SD 3.11) through a ONE-HOUR computer task with the CVS-Q administered pre and post. Scores rose from mean 6.76 (SD 3.76) to 9.21 (SD 4.57), p < .001; the proportion in the high-severity band (13–18) rose from 8% to 34%; the authors operationalised "deteriorated asthenopia" as a post-task increase of ≥3 points and used that as their reference standard [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2196/84479). This is a same-age, same-duration, pre-registered-power demonstration that within-session CVS-Q change is detectable. Its limits matter: n = 38, single site, single arm, no control condition, unblinded, and the identical habitual-recall stem was re-administered, so what actually shifted (symptom experience vs. salience/demand) is not separable. The same group used change-from-baseline CVS-Q as the PRIMARY outcome of a registered, double-masked RCT over 31 days, where the between-group effect was 1.75 points (95% CI 0.39–3.10) on the 0–32 scale [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/07853890.2026.2663105) — so realistic CVS-Q effects are small, roughly 1.5–3 raw points.

THREE PSYCHOMETRIC LIABILITIES YOU MUST DESIGN AROUND. (1) No responsiveness statistics exist. I found no published MCID, minimal detectable change, SEM or coefficient of repeatability for the CVS-Q. You cannot state that any observed change exceeds measurement error. By contrast the CVSS17 publishes a coefficient of repeatability of 8.14 (ICC 0.849) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1167/iovs.13-13818) and the new Rasch computer-adaptive DESCAQ publishes a repeatability limit of 13.35 (ICC 0.884, ~2 min to administer, validated 8–65 y in English and Spanish) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/opo.13540). (2) Poor low-end targeting. The developers repeatedly state the scale is "uninformative — both in psychometric and substantive terms — at the lower levels of the trait," with mean person severity roughly 2 logits below mean item severity; the Colombian version reports an 11.7% floor. Healthy 20–24-year-olds with normal vision sit precisely in that dead zone. (3) The raw sum is ordinal, not interval — the 0/1/2 recode of a 0–4 product compresses the top of the scale. The CVS-Q teen authors say so explicitly and give the remedy: the raw-to-logit conversion table "may be useful for researchers conducting clinical studies in which, for example, small variations in CVS are detected, and which require a higher precision of the instrument."

WHAT THE FIELD'S HIGHEST-AUTHORITY SOURCE SAYS. The TFOS Lifestyle Workshop report on the digital environment (Wolffsohn et al., The Ocular Surface, 2023; 128-study evidence base) states that digital eye strain has "no previously agreed definition/diagnostic criteria and limitations of current questionnaires which fail to differentiate such symptoms from those arising from non-digital tasks," and — directly relevant to your PRIMARY outcome, not just this claim — that "objective signs such as blink rate or critical flicker frequency changes are not 'diagnostic' of digital eye strain nor validated as sensitive" [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtos.2023.04.004). It also fixes the terminology: "digital eye strain (the preferred terminology)," defined as "the development or exacerbation of RECURRENT ocular symptoms and/or signs related specifically to digital device screen viewing" — a consensus definition built on recurrence, which is a further reason not to lean on it for a single 90-minute episode. Sheppard and Wolffsohn (BMJ Open Ophthalmology, 2018) had already warned that "correlations between objective and subjective measures are not always apparent" [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjophth-2018-000146). Pucker et al. (Clinical Optometry, 2024) confirm the CVS-Q and CVSS17 as "the two most used, validated DES questionnaires" [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2147/OPTO.S412382).

WHAT IS ACTUALLY APPROPRIATE FOR ACUTE, WITHIN-SESSION VISUAL FATIGUE. The right instrument class is the short state-anchored rating scale you already have. Chen, Chu, Huang, Ho and Nguyen (Scientific Reports, 2024; n = 48) used the six-item visual fatigue scale attributed to Heuer et al. — "It is hard for me to see / I have a strange feeling around my eyes / My eyes feel tired / I feel numb / I feel dizzy looking at the screen / I have a headache," each rated 1 ("not at all") to 10 ("extremely serious"), summed, and analysed as change from a within-session baseline — and it discriminated 15-minute from 30-minute exposures and posture × sex interactions [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-76334-9). Your protocol's own per-condition 5-item 0–10 fatigue scale is essentially this instrument, and it, not the CVS-Q, should carry the within-sitting hypotheses. (Caveat on provenance: I could NOT retrieve the original Heuer et al. paper through any tool in this session; cite it only after obtaining the primary source. Likewise I found no PubMed record for the Kennedy Simulator Sickness Questionnaire, so I make no claim about it.)

NASA-TLX. It is the field default: Dias, Ngo-Howard, Boskovski, Zenati and Yule's systematic review of 84 studies and 2053 participants found NASA-TLX the most-used self-report cognitive-load tool (44/84 studies, 52%) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/bjs.10795). On raw vs. weighted: Carswell et al. (Applied Ergonomics) scored the same dataset both ways — the traditional participant-specific weighted TLX and the "simplified procedure — the NASA Raw Task Load Index (NASA-RTLX) — using the unweighted mean of the subscale scores" — and found that "both administration formats and scoring methods were equally sensitive to task and repetition effects" (coefficients of equivalence r = 0.85 weighted, r = 0.81 raw) [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2010.06.003). Recent applied work reports the "raw unweighted form" as the default [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2647-1142). Use raw TLX. I could NOT retrieve Hart (2006) "NASA-TLX: 20 Years Later" or Hart & Staveland (1988) through any tool — nasa.gov, journals.sagepub.com and semanticscholar.org are all blocked by this session's egress proxy — so I do not cite them; obtain them from the primary source before citing. Separately, Dias et al. make the point that condemns your TLX PLACEMENT: self-report globals suit overall load, but "when the aim is to assess cognitive load related to specific operative stages, real-time tools should be used, as they allow capture of cognitive load fluctuation." A single TLX at session close cannot attribute workload to any of your 10 conditions; it will be dominated by recency (whichever condition the Williams square placed last) plus cumulative session fatigue.

NET VERDICT. "Validated instrument": yes, robustly, but in adult WORKERS, not in students, and your age band falls between the adult and teen validations. "For this design": only partly — the CVS-Q can be administered at most twice per sitting, so it can only ever address the BETWEEN-sitting illumination factor; it is structurally incapable of speaking to the within-sitting polarity × colour factors that are the core of the study. "A meaningful secondary outcome over ~90 minutes": defensible but not on the strength the claim asserts. It is a period-recall instrument whose reliability evidence is evidence of stability, it has no published minimal detectable change, and it floors in healthy young adults — yet one recent 19–31-year-old study did move it 2.45 points in 60 minutes. Treat it as an exploratory, session-level secondary with a re-anchored end-of-session stem and Rasch scoring, not as a headline outcome.

### Implications raised for this protocol

**must fix**

- **The protocol lists 'change in CVS-Q from baseline' as a secondary outcome without stating that the CVS-Q's frequency anchors and criterion validity are tied to a weekly-recurrence recall frame ('occurrence of at least one symptom two or three times a week'). Administered with its standard stem twice, 90 minutes apart, a consistent respondent should score identically; any difference is response drift, not visual fatigue.**
  Split the administration into two DIFFERENT stems and pre-register both. Baseline: keep the standard habitual-frequency stem verbatim - this is your case-mix / exposure descriptor and yields the ≥6 prevalence figure. Session close: re-anchor the stem to the episode, e.g. 'Thinking only about the time since you arrived today, how often did you experience each symptom during today's reading tasks?' Explicitly label this a modification of a validated instrument in the methods, state that the published cut-off, sensitivity, specificity and ICC do NOT transfer to the modified stem, and report the modified-stem score as a continuous exploratory measure only - never as a CVS 'case' rate.

- **The CVS-Q raw 0-32 sum is ordinal. The severity recode (product 0/1/2/4 collapsed to 0/1/2) deliberately compresses the top of the scale, so a one-point change at the bottom is not equivalent to a one-point change at the top. Analysing raw change with a paired t-test or a Gaussian mixed model is mis-specified.**
  Score on the Rasch logit metric, not the raw sum. The instrument developers state this explicitly for change studies: the raw-to-logit conversion table 'may be useful for researchers conducting clinical studies in which, for example, small variations in CVS are detected, and which require a higher precision of the instrument' (CVS-Q teen, Sci Rep 2024). Use the published conversion table for whichever version you administer, or fit your own Andrich rating-scale model in Winsteps/R (eRm, TAM) on your n=130 sample and analyse logit change. Report the raw sum only as a descriptive.

- **The CVS-Q can be administered at most twice per sitting (baseline and close). The within-sitting factors - 2 polarities x 5 text colours = 10 conditions - each occupy roughly 6-8 minutes inside the session. The CVS-Q change score is therefore structurally incapable of attributing anything to polarity or text colour; it can only ever contrast the two ambient-illumination sittings (10 lux vs 150 lux).**
  State this explicitly in the analysis plan and in the synopsis. Assign the CVS-Q change score to ONE hypothesis only: the between-sitting illumination effect (a paired contrast on 130 participants, with sitting order as a fixed effect). Remove or explicitly disclaim any implication that the CVS-Q informs the polarity x colour comparison. The within-sitting hypotheses must rest on the per-condition 5-item 0-10 fatigue scale and the per-condition blink metrics.

- **There is no published minimal clinically important difference, minimal detectable change, standard error of measurement or coefficient of repeatability for the CVS-Q. A targeted PubMed search for these terms returned nothing. You therefore cannot state that any observed change exceeds measurement error - which is exactly what a thesis examiner will ask.**
  Estimate your own from data you are already collecting for free. You take a baseline CVS-Q at sitting 1 and again at sitting 2, 48-72 h apart, both under no-exposure conditions - that is an embedded test-retest study with n=130, far larger than the 30-54 subsamples used in every published CVS-Q validation. Pre-specify: compute SEM = SD_diff / sqrt(2) from the two baselines, then MDC95 = 1.96 x sqrt(2) x SEM, and report any within-session change against that threshold. Publish it as a standalone methodological contribution - it is a genuine gap in the literature. As a secondary pre-specified threshold, use the ≥3-point increase that Song et al. (JMIR Hum Factors 2026) used in a 19-31 y sample over 60 minutes.

- **Floor effects are near-certain and unplanned-for. The developers state the CVS-Q is 'uninformative - both in psychometric and substantive terms - at the lower levels of the trait', with mean person severity roughly 2 logits below mean item severity; the Colombian validation reports an 11.7% floor. Healthy 20-24 y students with normal vision and Ishihara-screened colour vision are exactly this population - in the comparable Song et al. sample, 42% were below the ≥6 cut-off at baseline.**
  Pre-specify the handling before unblinding. (a) Report the proportion scoring 0 and the proportion below cut-off at each timepoint. (b) Do not use OLS on raw change. Use either a mixed-effects model on Rasch logits, or a censored/Tobit model, or an ordinal (cumulative-link) mixed model on the item responses. (c) Pre-register that if >20% of participants score 0 at baseline you will report the CVS-Q as descriptive only. (d) Do NOT exclude asymptomatic participants to avoid the floor - that would break the crossover balance and induce regression to the mean.

- **The synopsis asserts the CVS-Q is validated 'for this population'. Every adult validation was in video-display-terminal WORKERS (Italian n=241, mean age 45.5 y; Portuguese n=280 workers; Colombian n=300 university employees). The only youth version, CVS-Q teen, covers 12-17 y. A 20-24 y student sample falls between the two validated bands.**
  Rewrite the sentence to: 'The CVS-Q was developed and Rasch-validated in adult video-display-terminal workers (Seguí et al., 2015) and adapted and validated for adolescents aged 12-17 (CVS-Q teen, 2024). No validation exists specifically in university students aged 18-25; the adult version is used here on the basis that participants are adults, and this is acknowledged as a limitation.' Do not delete the instrument over this - just stop claiming what has not been shown.

- **The cut-off is not specified in the protocol, and cut-offs are version-specific: ≥6 for the original Spanish CVS-Q and for CVS-Q teen, but ≥7 for both the Italian (CVS-Q IT) and Portuguese (CVS-Q PT) versions.**
  Name the exact version you will administer and pin its cut-off in the protocol: if the original CVS-Q, use ≥6. Use the cut-off ONLY for baseline prevalence and case-mix description. Do NOT define the outcome as 'crossing the cut-off' - Song et al. used a ≥3-point CHANGE, not a threshold crossing, and dichotomising a change score at a diagnostic threshold throws away power and is sensitive to baseline position.

- **The scoring implementation has a known trap: the intensity scale has no 'none' category, so a symptom answered 'never' on frequency has an undefined intensity and an undefined product. The Colombian validation documents the fix used by the developers' own group.**
  Hard-code and unit-test the rule: if frequency = 0 ('never'), force intensity = 0 and severity = 0, bypassing the multiplication; otherwise severity_raw = frequency x intensity, then recode 0 to 0, 1 or 2 to 1, and 4 to 2; sum the 16 recoded severities for a 0-32 total. Add an assertion that no item can yield a value outside {0,1,2} and that the total never exceeds 32. Write a test case for every (frequency, intensity) cell including the impossible ones.

- **The single NASA-TLX at session close cannot be attributed to any of the 10 within-sitting display conditions. It will be dominated by recency (whichever condition the Williams square placed last) plus cumulative 90-minute fatigue. Dias et al. (Br J Surg 2018, 84 studies) state that for stage-specific load 'real-time tools should be used, as they allow capture of cognitive load fluctuation'.**
  Keep the TLX where it is, at session close, but demote and relabel it: 'session-level perceived workload, descriptive'. Delete any hypothesis that the TLX discriminates polarity or text colour. If workload genuinely is a hypothesis about display conditions, the fix is a single-item per-condition mental-effort probe (e.g. one 0-10 'how much mental effort did that passage take?' folded into the existing display-comfort/clarity ratings block, costing ~5 s per condition), NOT a 10x repetition of the full TLX, which would add roughly 10-15 minutes and contaminate the reading task.

- **Sitting order (first vs second illumination sitting) is perfectly confounded with practice, familiarity, and possible carryover in symptom reporting, for every session-level subjective outcome. The 48-72 h gap is short relative to the 7-15 day intervals over which CVS-Q test-retest stability was established, so a participant's baseline CVS-Q at sitting 2 may still carry symptoms attributable to sitting 1.**
  Add sitting order as a fixed effect to the split-plot model for every session-level outcome (CVS-Q change, NASA-TLX, baseline CVS-Q itself) and report the estimate rather than assuming counterbalancing has absorbed it. Additionally, pre-specify a formal carryover test: compare baseline CVS-Q at sitting 1 vs sitting 2 within participant. If sitting-2 baseline is significantly elevated, report it as evidence of carryover and widen the inter-sitting interval to at least 7 days in any follow-on work, matching the interval over which the instrument's stability is actually documented.

- **Power and effect-size expectations for the CVS-Q are unstated. The empirical benchmarks are small: a mean within-session rise of 2.45 raw points over 60 min in a 19-31 y sample (6.76 to 9.21, SDs 3.76 and 4.57), and a between-group effect of 1.75 points (95% CI 0.39-3.10) as the primary outcome of a 31-day double-masked RCT.**
  Power the illumination contrast on a paired difference of 1.5-2.5 raw CVS-Q points with a within-person SD of change of roughly 3.5-4.5 points (derived from the SDs above), or equivalently on the logit metric once you have your own conversion. At n=130 paired, a 2-point difference with SD_diff = 4 gives d ~ 0.5 and >99% power at alpha .05 - so the CVS-Q contrast is not the binding constraint; say so, and do not over-claim precision that the instrument's targeting cannot deliver.

- **Raw vs weighted NASA-TLX is unspecified. The 15 pairwise-comparison weighting step costs several minutes at the end of an already 90-minute session.**
  Use RAW TLX: the unweighted mean of the six subscale ratings, and say so in the methods ('NASA-TLX, raw unweighted form'). Drop the pairwise weighting entirely. Carswell et al. (Appl Ergon) scored the same data both ways and found them 'equally sensitive to task and repetition effects' (coefficients of equivalence r = 0.85 weighted vs 0.81 raw). Report the six subscales individually as well as the global mean - Mental Demand and Effort are the ones that could plausibly differ by display condition, and averaging them with Physical Demand dilutes the signal.

- **All subjective outcomes are unblinded and expectancy-prone. Display polarity is visible by definition, the study's purpose is transparent to a 20-24 y student cohort ('does dark mode tire your eyes?'), and participants complete a fatigue scale after every one of 10 conditions plus a CVS-Q and TLX at close. None of the retrieved within-session studies were participant-blinded either.**
  Pre-register this explicitly rather than discovering it at viva. Concretely: (a) state in the limitations that participant blinding to polarity is impossible and that subjective outcomes are therefore susceptible to demand characteristics; (b) use a neutral participant-facing framing in the information sheet ('a study of reading on screens') that does not name display polarity as the hypothesis; (c) add one post-session item asking what participants believed the study was testing, and report the proportion who guessed correctly as a demand-characteristics check; (d) pre-specify that the subjective and objective outcome families are reported separately and that convergence between them is treated as corroboration, not as validation of either.

- **The synopsis appears to treat blink metrics as an objective gold standard against which the subjective measures are judged. The TFOS Lifestyle report states the opposite: 'Objective signs such as blink rate or critical flicker frequency changes are not "diagnostic" of digital eye strain nor validated as sensitive', and Sheppard & Wolffsohn note that 'correlations between objective and subjective measures are not always apparent'.**
  Reframe the outcome hierarchy in the synopsis: incomplete-blink ratio is a mechanistic physiological outcome in its own right, NOT a proxy for or validator of symptomatic digital eye strain, and the subjective scales are not a validity check on it. Add a sentence citing the TFOS position so the examiner sees you know it. Pre-specify that a dissociation between blink metrics and symptom ratings is an anticipated and reportable result, not a failure - this protects you from a null on either side.

- **Terminology and definition. The protocol uses 'visual fatigue' and CVS interchangeably. The 2023 TFOS Lifestyle consensus fixes 'digital eye strain' as 'the preferred terminology' and defines it as 'the development or exacerbation of RECURRENT ocular symptoms and/or signs related specifically to digital device screen viewing'.**
  Adopt 'digital eye strain (DES)' as the primary term throughout, noting 'computer vision syndrome (CVS)' as the synonym retained because the instrument is named for it, and quote the TFOS definition once in the introduction with the citation. Then draw the explicit inference in the discussion of measures: because the consensus definition is built on RECURRENCE, a single 90-minute exposure is by definition not a test of DES incidence, and the CVS-Q change is best read as acute symptom provocation rather than as a change in DES status.

- **The per-condition 5-item 0-10 visual-fatigue scale - the instrument that actually carries the within-sitting hypotheses - has no stated provenance, item wording, or validation basis in the synopsis.**
  Anchor it to the published six-item visual fatigue scale as operationalised by Chen et al. (Sci Rep 2024): items rated 1 ('not at all') to 10 ('extremely serious'), summed, and analysed as change from a within-session baseline; that design detected 15-min vs 30-min differences with n=48. Two concrete changes to your protocol: (i) take a fatigue-scale reading at session baseline AND immediately after the neutral-grey adaptation field preceding the FIRST condition, giving a within-session anchor measured under a known-neutral adaptation state, so per-condition scores are differences from a state baseline rather than from a pre-session baseline contaminated by arrival arousal; (ii) report per-item scores as well as the sum, because the dry-eye-linked and accommodative/binocular-linked symptom clusters dissociate. Obtain and cite the original Heuer et al. source before naming it - it could not be retrieved in this literature search.

- **Version control and language. No validated CVS-Q exists in Assamese, Bengali or Hindi according to this search, and the Chinese adaptation demonstrates that a translation without psychometric validation forfeits the cut-off and the reliability estimates.**
  Decide and document one of three options in the protocol: (a) administer the published English CVS-Q, restrict enrolment to participants who self-report English reading fluency (already implied by a 600-word English passage), and add a one-item comprehension check on the questionnaire itself; (b) run a proper forward/back-translation and Rasch adaptation as a declared sub-study, which would be a publishable contribution; or (c) drop the CVS-Q. Do not administer an informal translation and report the ≥6 cut-off against it.

- **Multiplicity across a large secondary-outcome family. The protocol lists at least eleven secondary outcomes (CVS-Q change, blink rate, inter-blink interval, PERCLOS, long closures, reading speed, comprehension, search hits, false positives, RT, d-prime, subjective fatigue, comfort, NASA-TLX), each crossed with 2 polarities x 5 colours x 2 illuminations.**
  Pre-register an outcome hierarchy: one primary (incomplete-blink ratio), a small confirmatory secondary family (name 3-4, with a stated multiplicity correction such as Holm or a hierarchical gatekeeping order), and everything else declared exploratory and reported with unadjusted estimates plus confidence intervals and no significance claims. Put the CVS-Q change in the exploratory tier, for the reasons above. Register the plan on OSF or as a protocol paper before data collection.

THE HALF OF THE CLAIM THAT IS WELL SUPPORTED: the mechanism. The literature converges strongly on the position that what drives ocular load at a display is the luminance relationship between the emitting screen and the field the eye adapts to — not room illuminance considered alone. The most directly relevant evidence is Lin M et al. (2025, Contact Lens & Anterior Eye), which crossed ambient illuminance (bright/dark room) with screen brightness (bright/dark screen) in 30 young adults over 30-minute phone reading and measured the very outcome this protocol uses as its primary endpoint. Neither room nor screen alone was the story: the dark-room + bright-screen cell had significantly the worst incomplete blink rate and partial blink ratio (P<0.05) and the worst subjective fatigue versus bright-room + bright-screen (P<0.01), and incomplete-blink frequency correlated with fatigue score (r=0.29, P=0.03). That is a room x screen interaction on incomplete blinking, i.e. exactly the mechanism the claim asserts, demonstrated on exactly this protocol's primary outcome. Rodán et al. (2025, Vision) reach the same framing from the physiology side: they deliberately operationalised their two ambient conditions as "low" and "high luminance CONTRAST RATIO" (295 lx photopic vs 0.05 lx mesopic, with the monitor as the primary light source), found greater accommodative variability under the high-ratio/mesopic condition (F(1,31)=7.697, p=0.009, eta-p2=0.199), and — counterintuitively — found pupils SMALLER under the mesopic condition (F(1,30)=4.917, p=0.034, eta-p2=0.141), because in a dark room the screen itself dominates the adaptation state. Chawla & Samei (2007, Medical Physics) formalise this: they model a global luminance adaptation value from the display, convert room illuminance E into surround luminance via the surface diffuse reflection coefficient, and then minimise the adaptation mismatch — concluding that room illuminance of 75–150 lux with surface reflection coefficients of 0.13–0.22 sr^-1 is the optimum for typical LCDs. Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner (2014, Ergonomics; 2014, Human Factors) supply the polarity half of the mechanism: positive polarity produces smaller pupils and better proofreading, and the positive-polarity advantage grows linearly as character size shrinks — i.e. polarity acts THROUGH mean display luminance. Lin C, Ji & Lin (2024, Ergonomics) and Lin C et al. (2022, Optics Express) close the loop by fitting the optimal display luminance as a function of ambient illuminance (an inverted-U giving 153 cd/m2 under 300 lx; a power-function luminance-adjustment model across 10^0–10^4 lx). Yoon, Kwak & Kim (2025, Optics Express) build their whole model on "the luminance ratio between the display and ambient light" and an explicit "surround ratio". And the standards world specifies LUMINANCE, not illuminance: the HFES 100 draft I extracted requires a display capable of at least 35 cd/m2 and recommends at least 100 cd/m2, notes legibility falls below 35 cd/m2, and sets a minimum contrast ratio of 3.

THE HALF THAT DOES NOT HOLD: the specific levels, and their labels. "150 lux = moderate" is not defensible against any office standard. Web-sourced secondary reporting of EN 12464-1:2021 puts maintained task illuminance for a standard office workstation at 500 lx (raised to 750–1000 lx for older occupants or fine detail). Comparable reading-and-asthenopia experiments sit in the 300–1000 lx band: Chen et al. (2022/2023, Ophthalmic & Physiological Optics; n=22, 2-hour reading, subjective + optical-quality + tear-cytokine outcomes) tested 300/500/750/1000 lx and found 500 lx–4000 K produced the LEAST asthenopia while 300 lx–2700 K was among the worst — 150 lx is below the lowest level they considered worth testing. Lin C et al. (2024) treat 300 lx as "office lighting level". Where 150 lux DOES appear in the recent literature it appears as the TOP of a deliberately low range: Atuanya et al. (2025, Clinical Optometry; n=30) tested 50/100/150 lux for near positive fusional vergence, with large effect sizes (rank-biserial -0.798 to -1.000) across that span; and Chawla & Samei's 75–150 lux is a recommendation for DARKENED radiology reading rooms. So 150 lux is honestly "low / dim-office / reading-room upper bound", not "moderate". Calling it moderate in a synopsis is the single most examinable error here. As for 10 lux: it is realistic as a night/bedroom condition and it is what the closest comparator study used — Fan et al. (2024, Sensors; n=50, negative polarity, five text colours, 0/5/10 lux, eye tracker) — but note two things from that paper's full text. First, they justified 10 lux by citing a NIGHT-DRIVING road-visibility study, not any reading study. Second, and more awkward for this protocol, ambient illuminance across 0–10 lux had essentially no effect on their outcomes that matter here: on cognitive performance F(2,147)=0.319, p>0.05, eta-p2=0.005 (versus text colour F(4,145)=117.999, eta-p2=0.257), and Bonferroni comparisons across 0, 5 and 10 lux showed NO significant differences in the visual fatigue index, and no significant text-colour x ambient interaction on visual fatigue. The 10-vs-150 lux span is much wider than 0-vs-10, so this is not fatal — but it is a published null from the nearest neighbour and a reviewer will find it.

THE HEADLINE INTERACTION IS CONTESTED, AND THE STRONGEST CONTRADICTION IS OLD AND WELL-CITED. Buchner & Baumgartner (2007, Ergonomics) ran a series of proofreading experiments and found the positive-polarity advantage was INDEPENDENT of ambient lighting (darkness vs typical office illumination) and independent of chromaticity, and that physiological strain measures (breathing rate, heart rate, HRV, skin conductance) and self-reported fatigue, eyestrain and headache did not vary with any independent variable. That is a direct, well-powered denial of the polarity x ambient interaction this protocol is built on. Supporting the interaction: Sethi & Ziat (2023, Ergonomics) crossed polarity with bright/dim environments and age, and found negative polarity raised cognitive load (search time, pupil diameter) for older adults in a BRIGHT environment and for younger adults in a DIM environment — note the direction is age-dependent, and this protocol's sample is 20–24-year-olds, i.e. exactly the group for whom the cost of negative polarity appeared in the DIM condition. Also contradicting a large polarity effect on fatigue specifically: Sengsoon & Intaruk (2025, IJERPH), a randomised block trial in 30 tablet users, found NO significant difference in visual fatigue between light and dark mode (p>0.05), though critical flicker frequency and dry-eye symptoms did differ. The polarity ADVANTAGE for performance, by contrast, replicates robustly and recently, including into video see-through mixed reality (Luzsa & Mayr, 2025, Ergonomics) and across age (Piepenbrock et al., 2013, Ergonomics). Gazit et al. (2025, Ergonomics; n=173 online) also found higher cognitive scores in light mode. Finally, the authoritative recent synthesis — Wolffsohn et al. (2023, The Ocular Surface, TFOS Lifestyle report on the digital environment) — names "reduced blink rate and completeness" plus "differences in position, size, brightness and glare" as the mechanisms of digital eye strain, which endorses the protocol's outcome choice and the brightness framing, while cautioning that objective signs such as blink rate are neither diagnostic of digital eye strain nor validated as sensitive.

SHOULD THE PROTOCOL MEASURE cd/m2 RATHER THAN LUX? Yes, and it should measure both. Lux at the eye tells you nothing about what the eye is adapted to without the reflectance of the surfaces behind and around the tablet — that conversion (surround luminance = illuminance x reflectance / pi) is precisely the step Chawla & Samei make explicit, and it is why their recommendation is stated jointly as an illuminance range AND a reflectance range. The standards specify luminance ratios, not illuminance: web-sourced reporting of the IESNA handbook gives 3:1 task-to-surround and 10:1 across the whole field of view, with display-to-immediate-surround around 1:3 and the wider field 5–10x the display; the HFES 100 draft I read specifies absolute display luminance in cd/m2 and a contrast ratio of 3. A protocol whose stated mechanism is "the room-to-screen luminance relationship" but whose only photometric independent variable is room illuminance cannot test that mechanism.

THE UNSTATED CONFOUND THAT MATTERS MOST. Display polarity IS a screen-luminance manipulation. A full-page positive-polarity render emits roughly an order of magnitude more light than a negative-polarity render on the same panel at the same brightness setting (Fan et al. ran their negative-polarity screen at 28 cd/m2, 5% of maximum). So the protocol's "headline interaction" of polarity x ambient illumination is, physically, a screen-luminance x surround-luminance interaction wearing different clothes — which is fine, and is arguably the more interesting hypothesis, but it must be stated that way and the luminances must be measured, or a reviewer will read the interaction as uninterpretable. There is a second, sharper problem: at 10 lux the participant's face is illuminated almost entirely BY THE TABLET SCREEN. The screen's output varies systematically with polarity and with text colour. Therefore the illumination available to the front-camera FaceMesh pipeline — and hence frame rate, landmark confidence, and EAR noise — is confounded with the independent variables, in the arm where the primary outcome is measured. The protocol already gates duration-based blink measures below ~25 fps, which is the right instinct, but that gate will fire non-randomly: preferentially in negative-polarity, dark-text-colour, 10-lux cells. Elkhodiry et al. (2026, BMC Psychiatry), a scoping review of 90 webcam/home eye-tracking studies, lists ambient-light variability first among practical barriers to camera-based ocular measurement. This is a measurement-validity threat to the primary endpoint, not a nuisance.

### Implications raised for this protocol

**must fix**

- **'150 lux (moderate)' is mislabelled. No office lighting standard puts a moderate reading level at 150 lux; secondary reporting of EN 12464-1:2021 gives 500 lx for office task areas, Lin C et al. (2024) treat 300 lx as the reference 'office lighting level', and Chen et al. (2022) did not test below 300 lx in an 8-condition reading-asthenopia study. Where 150 lux appears in current work it is the TOP of a low range (Atuanya et al. 2025: 50/100/150 lux) or the ceiling of a darkened-reading-room recommendation (Chawla & Samei 2007: 75-150 lux). A viva examiner will ask what 150 lux is moderate relative to.**
  Do one of two things, and say which in the synopsis. PREFERRED: move the high level to 300 lx, matching Lin C, Ji & Lin (2024) so the protocol's polarity findings are directly comparable to the only recent study that fitted optimum display luminance against polarity at an office level; or to 500 lx to match EN 12464-1 task illuminance and the Chen et al. (2022) optimum. IF 150 lx must be retained for site feasibility, relabel the two levels as '10 lx (mesopic, night-time domestic)' and '150 lx (dim, upper bound of the recommended darkened display-reading-room range per Chawla & Samei 2007)', and add one explicit sentence to the limitations stating that no office-standard illuminance was sampled, so the results do not generalise to standards-compliant workplaces.

- **The protocol's stated mechanism (room-to-screen luminance relationship) is not measured by any variable it collects. It logs illuminance in lux at the eye and nothing in cd/m2. Illuminance cannot be converted to the surround luminance the eye adapts to without the reflectance of the surfaces around the tablet — that conversion is the entire method of Chawla & Samei (2007), whose recommendation is stated jointly as an illuminance range AND a reflectance range (0.13-0.22 sr^-1) precisely because illuminance alone is indeterminate.**
  Add three photometric measurements to the session protocol. (1) With a spot luminance meter, measure and record screen luminance in cd/m2 for the full-page white state and full-page black state of ALL TEN polarity x colour conditions, at the exact brightness setting used, once per lighting sitting (20 readings per sitting). (2) Measure the luminance in cd/m2 of the surface immediately behind and around the tablet at the participant's eye position, at start, middle and end, alongside the existing lux readings. (3) If no luminance meter is available, measure the diffuse reflectance rho of the desk and the wall behind the tablet once, and compute surround luminance as L = E x rho / pi, reporting rho in the methods. Then derive, per condition, the area-weighted mean screen luminance for the page AS RENDERED (not the white state — a page of text is mostly background) and the screen:surround luminance ratio.

- **Display polarity IS a screen-luminance manipulation, so the headline 'polarity x ambient illumination' interaction is physically a screen-luminance x surround-luminance interaction. Fan et al. (2024) ran their negative-polarity screen at 28 cd/m2, 5% of maximum; a positive-polarity page on the same panel emits roughly an order of magnitude more. Piepenbrock et al. (2014) show the polarity effect operates THROUGH display luminance. As specified, the protocol cannot tell whether an interaction is about polarity or about luminance.**
  Pick a lane and pre-register it. OPTION A (cheap, honest): reframe the hypothesis explicitly as 'display polarity acts on ocular load through mean screen luminance, and its effect is moderated by the screen:surround luminance ratio', and enter log10(screen:surround luminance ratio) as a continuous predictor in the mixed model ALONGSIDE the categorical polarity and illuminance factors. This converts the mechanism from an assumption into a testable term. OPTION B (stronger, costlier): add a luminance-matched control block in which negative-polarity mean page luminance is raised (or positive-polarity lowered) to match, for at least one text colour, so polarity can be dissociated from luminance within-participant. Without one of these, Buchner & Baumgartner (2007) — who found the polarity effect independent of ambient lighting — cannot be answered.

- **MEASUREMENT-VALIDITY THREAT TO THE PRIMARY OUTCOME. At 10 lux the participant's face is illuminated almost entirely by the tablet screen. Screen output varies systematically with polarity (about 10x) and with text colour. Therefore the illumination reaching the front-camera FaceMesh pipeline — and hence frame rate, landmark confidence and EAR noise — is confounded with the independent variables, in the arm where the primary endpoint (incomplete blink ratio) is measured. The existing ~25 fps gate for duration-based measures will fire non-randomly: preferentially in negative-polarity, dark-text-colour, 10-lux cells, producing informative missingness. Elkhodiry et al. (2026) list ambient-light variability as the leading practical barrier to camera-based ocular tracking.**
  Log, per condition, the median and 10th-percentile frame rate, the median MediaPipe landmark confidence, the proportion of frames yielding a valid EAR, and the mean open-eye EAR baseline. Pre-register these as condition-level data-quality covariates and report a sensitivity analysis with them included. Then run a bench pilot BEFORE recruitment: with no participant changes, capture the same face under all 10 conditions at 10 lux and at 150 lux and confirm that frame rate and landmark confidence do not differ systematically by condition. ## Addendum: the CVS-Q recall window — targeted search, and what was changed

This section is later and narrower than the rest of the file, and its retrieval status is
different, so it is set out separately.

**What was asked.** What recall window does the CVS-Q specify, and is a within-session change score
a defensible use of it?

**What could NOT be confirmed.** Seguí et al. (2015) is closed access and could not be retrieved:
the publisher, Europe PMC and the author institution's repository were all unreachable from this
session. **The published question stem has not been read verbatim and is not reproduced here.**

**What WAS established, from sources that were retrieved.** Two facts settle the question without
needing the stem, and both come from papers co-authored by the instrument's developers or from
primary validations of its translations:

1. **The frequency anchors are defined in events per week** — "never: the symptom does not occur at
   all"; "occasionally: sporadic episodes or once a week"; "often or always: 2 or 3 times a week to
   almost every day".
2. **The case criterion is "occurrence of at least one symptom two or three times a week"**, stated
   identically in the Italian, Persian and adolescent validations, each attributing it to the
   original.

And the test-retest design confirms the intent: intervals of 7-15 days across the Italian,
Portuguese and teen versions, with the Italian paper reporting "no differences ... between the mean
scores obtained before and after (p = 0.440) ... good stability over time". **The retest exists to
show the score does not move.** The shortest repeated administration found anywhere in the family is
one week. No published use of a CVS-Q change score across a single session was found in PubMed,
Scite or Consensus — absence of evidence across three databases, not proof of absence.

**No MCID or MDC has been published for the CVS-Q.** The only raw-score SEM retrieved is 1.915
(Persian version), which would imply an MDC95 of roughly 5 points on the 0-32 scale — about a sixth
of the range — for a construct its own validation shows to be stable over a fortnight. That
arithmetic is this project's, not a published figure.

### What was changed in the instrument

The screen previously asked, at BOTH administrations, "How often, and how strongly, have you felt
each symptom?" — with no period at all, which matches no validated version. That is worse than
either horn of the dilemma: a participant reading it habitually reported the same thing twice and
produced a change of zero, while one reading it as present-state produced a change of several
points, and nothing in the export recorded which reading they had used.

The frame is now explicit and differs by stage:

- **Baseline** carries the validated habitual frame, anchored to computer work, with the per-week
  anchor definitions shown to the participant.
- **Session close** is deliberately re-anchored to the session just completed, with anchors that
  mean something over ninety minutes.

Each row records which frame was used, in a new `frame` column on `13_cvsq.csv`, and the codebook
states that a `this_session` total must not be read against the published cut-off or against
published norms.

### The decision this does NOT make

Re-anchoring makes the closing administration a coherent question. It does not make it the CVS-Q,
and a baseline-to-close difference is now explicitly a difference between two different questions.
The recommendation from the search is to **demote the CVS-Q to a screening-time covariate** — used
for what it was validated to do, case definition and stratification — and to add a purpose-built
acute instrument as the pre/post endpoint. The candidate with the clearest supporting evidence is
the VRSQ (Ames, Wolffsohn & McBrien 2005), which was explicitly validated for pre/post differencing,
with the caveat from that paper that symptoms dissipate and the post measure must be taken within
five minutes of task end. Singh, Downie & Anderson (2022) is the closest precedent for a
within-session symptom change in diagnosed CVS patients over a two-hour task.

That is a change to the study's key secondary outcome and is the candidate's and supervisor's to
make, so it has not been made here. If the within-session CVS-Q change is retained, the searches
suggest reporting it as exploratory, scoring it in Rasch logits using the published conversion
tables rather than raw sums, and stating the stability-by-design limitation explicitly.
