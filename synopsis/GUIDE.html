00 — OrientationThe whole study in one page

Covers · title, aim, §1.6

People read on screens for hours a day, and roughly seven in ten of them report tired, dry or aching eyes as a result. Almost every treatment sold for this — blue-light lenses above all — fails in trials. But the stimulus itself is free to change: whether text is dark-on-light or light-on-dark, what colour the letters are, and how bright the room is. Nobody has properly tested those three things together.

This study puts 130 young adults in a light-controlled room, gives each of them a tablet, and has them read under twenty different display setups — ten colour-and-polarity combinations, each seen twice, once in dim light and once in ordinary indoor light. While they read, the tablet's own camera counts their blinks and, crucially, works out how many of those blinks failed to close all the way. That single number, the incomplete-blink ratio, is the study's primary outcome, because incompletely closing the eyelid is the specific step that lets the tear film break down.

01 — The problemWhy any of this matters

Covers · §1.1 Background · §1.4 Statement of the problem · §2.1 Burden

Reading moved onto screens, and screens are not paper

A printed page is passive. It reflects whatever light falls on it, and once it is printed its appearance is fixed. A screen is active. It makes its own light, and essentially every property of what it shows can be changed in software — instantly, by the designer who built the app or by the user who flips a theme switch.

That difference is the opening the study walks through. If appearance is a software setting, then appearance is a modifiable risk factor, and finding the best setting costs nothing to deploy afterwards.

The condition has a name, and it is common

When prolonged screen use produces dry, tired, sore or gritty eyes, blurred vision, difficulty refocusing, headache and neck ache together, the cluster is labelled digital eye strain — or, interchangeably, computer vision syndrome. The synopsis uses the first name for the condition and the second only where a cited study or questionnaire uses it.

The headline number and what sits behind it

A meta-analysis of 103 cross-sectional studies covering 66,577 people put pooled prevalence at 69.0% (95% CI 62.3–75.3). Individual studies ranged from 12.1% to 97.3% — a spread so wide it is itself a finding.

Higher in women (71.4% vs 61.8%) and in university students (76.1%). Reviews report 50–60% among children.

Why the spread? Because there is no agreed case definition. Studies that used the validated Computer Vision Syndrome Questionnaire reported lower prevalence than those using home-made checklists (75.4% without it). Ad-hoc checklists appear to inflate the estimate. This is why the present study uses a validated instrument with a published cut-off rather than inventing its own symptom list.

The treatments do not work

This is the part that turns a nuisance into a research problem. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 45 randomised controlled trials with 4,497 participants found no high-certainty evidence for any therapy examined, blue-blocking lenses included. The only signal was low-certainty benefit from oral omega-3 for dry-eye symptoms. A separate review in younger users reached the same verdict on blue-light filters and recommended ergonomic optimisation and shorter exposure instead.

So: a condition affecting most screen users, no working treatment, and an explicit recommendation to optimise ergonomics — while the most basic ergonomic variables of all remain untested for prolonged reading. That is the gap this study occupies.

What a clinician cannot currently answer

The synopsis states the practical consequence plainly: a clinician, designer or standards body has no evidence base from which to advise how a tablet should be configured for long reading, or how that configuration should change with the light in the room. Everyone has an opinion about dark mode. Nobody has the data.

02 — How eyes tireTwo separate routes, and why separating them matters

Covers · §1.2 Biophysiological basis · §1.9 Conceptual framework, Table 1.1

The symptoms listed above do not all arise the same way. Two mechanisms are involved, and telling them apart is what makes the study measurable — because each route needs a different instrument.

Route one: focusing effort

To read something at arm's length, the eye has to bend light more strongly than it does for distance. It achieves this by changing the shape of its internal lens; this is called accommodation. At the same time, both eyes must swivel slightly inwards so the two images fuse into one, which is vergence. Holding both for a long stretch is effortful work, and when the effort accumulates you get difficulty focusing, blurred vision, headache, and a temporary drift towards short-sightedness after close work — near-work-induced transient myopia.

Scope, stated honestly

This study does not measure accommodation. Doing so needs open-field autorefractometry, which is lab-bench instrumentation and outside the scope here. The focusing route is reviewed as mechanistic context — it explains why polarity should matter — but it is not tested. The synopsis says so explicitly rather than leaving the omission to be discovered.

Route two: the ocular surface

The exposed front of the eye is kept smooth and comfortable by a thin tear film, which is renewed every time the eyelids close fully. A blink does two jobs: it spreads fresh tear fluid across the surface, and it drags an oily secretion from glands in the lid margins over the top of that fluid, which slows evaporation.

Concentrated screen work interferes with both jobs. It reduces how often people blink, and — more importantly — it increases the proportion of blinks in which the lids approach each other but never actually meet. These are incomplete blinks. An incomplete blink leaves part of the surface unwetted and unprotected, so the tear film thins and breaks up sooner, and the surface enters the recognised inflammatory cycle of dry eye disease.

The single most important sentence in the study's logic

It is the completeness of blinking, not how often blinking happens, that governs tear-film stability. That is why the incomplete-blink ratio is the primary outcome, and why blink rate alone is treated as a secondary measure that can mislead.

Mechanistic review attributes display-associated dry eye principally to impaired blinking, and finds insufficient support for the two popular alternative explanations — parasympathetic nervous-system effects and blue light.

Why the primary outcome is a ratio and not a count

Three findings in the literature, taken together, forbid reading blink rate as a simple fatigue index:

  1. Reading suppresses blinking whatever the medium. Blink rate falls on paper as well as on screens, so a low rate does not indicate a display problem.
  2. Rate can hold steady while quality collapses. In a 60-minute study, tear break-up time fell from 8.62 s to 4.33 s at 30 minutes and then recovered as incomplete blinking declined — with total rate not telling that story.
  3. Raising blink rate experimentally did not help. Participants prompted to blink more often showed no change in symptom score.

The proportion of blinks that fail to close, referenced to each participant's own baseline, survives all three objections. It is also the measure with a documented symptom correlation: in one 15-minute reading study, incomplete blinks ranged from 0.9% to 56.5% of all blinks (mean 16.1%), and symptoms correlated with that percentage at p = 0.002.

03 — The three factorsPolarity, colour and room light

Covers · §1.1 definitions · §1.3 · §2.2 Table 2.1 · §2.3 Table 2.2 · §2.4 Table 2.3

Four words you need before anything else

Display polarity
Which of the two elements — letters or background — is the lighter one. Positive polarity is dark text on a light background, the arrangement of a printed book. Negative polarity is light text on a dark background, popularly known as dark mode.
Text colour (chromaticity)
The hue of the letters themselves. Letters need not be black or white; they can be blue, red, yellow, green.
Luminance
How much light the screen actually emits, measured in candelas per square metre (cd/m²).
Luminance contrast
The brightness difference between the letters and their background. This is the property that determines whether text is easy or hard to resolve. It is not the same as colour difference: two colours can look completely different and still be hard to read against one another.
The distinction the whole study turns on

Colour difference and brightness difference are different things. Red text on a green background is chromatically dramatic and nearly illegible, because the two have similar luminance. Proofreading experiments confirmed directly that colour contrast cannot compensate for absent luminance contrast. Hold on to this — it is why Part 07 exists.

Factor one: polarity, and the best-replicated finding in the field

The positive polarity advantage is the finding that reading is measurably better for dark-on-light than for light-on-dark. It replicates across paradigms, tasks and display technologies. The accepted explanation is a chain of four steps, and it runs entirely through the pupil:

Seven studies in the synopsis (Table 2.1) support the advantage. Two features of that corpus define where this study starts:

  • The tasks are short and threshold-limited. Proofreading, lexical decision, adaptive-staircase psychophysics, glance reading. None measures blinking, validated symptoms, comprehension or fatigue accumulated over an hour.
  • The stimuli are almost entirely achromatic. Black-on-white versus white-on-black. Coloured text barely appears.
The inference everyone makes and nobody has earned

An advantage in legibility does not license a conclusion about visual fatigue. Popular argument about dark mode makes that leap constantly, in both directions. Bridging brief legibility work to hour-scale fatigue is one of this study's specific contributions.

And the advantage is not universal: one study found the cognitive cost of negative polarity depended jointly on the reader's age and on the room lighting. Polarity effects are conditional, which is an argument for crossing polarity with other factors rather than testing it alone.

Factor two: text colour, where the evidence thins out

Only five studies (Table 2.2) manipulate text chromaticity, and none crosses colour with polarity while measuring fatigue. Two limitations recur across all of them:

  1. Exposure is brief. A study with 20 participants over 2-minute passages found colour changed accommodative and pupillary dynamics but left reading speed alone; a study measuring fatigue found strong colour effects. Duration is a plausible explanation for the disagreement, and neither study was designed to estimate it.
  2. Hue is never separated from luminance contrast. Because coloured pigments differ intrinsically in brightness, any fixed set of colours rendered on a light and a dark field produces unequal, polarity-asymmetric contrasts. Contrast is an inseparable travelling companion of any colour manipulation — so an apparent colour effect may simply be a contrast effect wearing a costume.

One study adds a complication that this design must confront directly: its objective and subjective measures pointed in opposite directions. Dark mode improved the blink measures, yet light mode was preferred and rated less fatiguing. The present study treats that subjective–objective discrepancy as an object of study rather than an inconvenience, which is why it co-registers a validated questionnaire with the camera metrics in the same condition.

Factor three: ambient light, the moderator

Room light matters because it drives pupil size independently of the screen, and it adds veiling luminance to the display surface. If the polarity advantage really works through the pupil, then the amount of light already in the room should change how much the advantage is worth. Six studies (Table 2.3) bear on this, and they are not unanimous:

04 — The gapWhat nobody has done

Covers · §1.4 · §2.5 Table 2.4 · §2.7 · §2.8 Research gaps

The synopsis lists six gaps. They are easier to hold in mind as three kinds of missing thing.

Missing combinations

  • Polarity is established for brief achromatic legibility tasks, untested for prolonged reading with fatigue accumulation and ocular-surface outcomes.
  • The polarity × colour interaction on fatigue has not been estimated: the only text-colour fatigue study held polarity constant.
  • Three-way effects of polarity, colour and illumination are uncharacterised, and the available two-way findings partly conflict.

Missing separation

  • Hue has never been separated from luminance contrast in any of the located studies.
  • No study reports contrast metrics alongside measured display photometry — the numbers are asserted, not measured.
  • The prevailing accessibility standard is polarity-blind, which is a problem when polarity is among the strongest determinants of legibility.

Missing measurement

  • The incomplete-blink ratio has never been used as the outcome of a display-parameter manipulation, despite being the most specific marker available.
  • Few studies co-register a validated symptom instrument with objective markers in the same condition; where both were measured they pointed in opposite directions.
  • Individual differences — habitual dark-mode preference, exposure, lighting environment — are seldom modelled as moderators.
The bottleneck that kept this field small

Every study in Table 2.4 measured blink completeness by manual frame-by-frame video coding or with dedicated hardware. That is why their samples are 11, 12, 21, 25, 30 and 50 participants. Annotation labour, not scientific interest, is the constraint.

Removing that bottleneck is Objective 4, and it is why the study is worth a methodological objective alongside its three substantive ones. No published study has validated a camera-based incomplete-blink measure against manual annotation.

The eye-aspect-ratio choice deserves one more sentence, because it explains why a tablet camera is defensible here when it would not be for other purposes. Camera-based gaze estimation is genuinely inferior to infrared tracking in both spatial accuracy and sampling rate — so gaze is used only for coarse zone classification. But blink measures are proportions, and proportions need temporal rather than spatial precision. Blinks last 100–400 ms, so duration-based metrics become unreliable below roughly 25 frames per second, while proportion measures hold up. The study exploits that asymmetry deliberately and gates the duration-dependent metrics on the frame rate actually achieved.

05 — Aim & hypothesesExactly what is being asked

Covers · §1.6 Aim · §1.7 Objectives · §1.8 Hypotheses

The aim, unpacked

The formal aim is one long sentence. It contains two halves:

Substantive half. To determine the effects and interactions of display polarity, text colour and ambient illumination on visual fatigue, ocular behaviour and visual task performance during prolonged tablet-based reading.

Methodological half. And, as a secondary objective, to evaluate the validity and feasibility of camera-based blink assessment as an objective measure of ocular response.

Why H₁ᵪ is the cleverest part of the design

Hue and contrast cannot be pulled apart experimentally — you cannot change a letter's colour without changing its brightness. So the design does something else: it uses the fact that the contrast ordering reverses between the two polarities to make the two explanations predict opposite patterns.

If contrast is doing the work, the best colour on white must be among the worst on black. If hue is doing the work, the best colour stays best in both. One experiment, two rival predictions, and they cannot both be right. Part 07 shows why the reversal is guaranteed.

The formal null and alternative

H₀ — display polarity, text colour and ambient illumination, individually or in combination, have no significant effect on visual fatigue, ocular behaviour or visual task performance during prolonged tablet reading. H₁ — at least one of these factors, individually or in combination, significantly affects these outcomes.

06 — The designA crossover, and what "split-plot" means

Covers · §3.1 Study design, Figure 3.1 · §3.2 setting · Figure 3.2

The design is a repeated-measures crossover (split-plot) factorial. That phrase carries three separate ideas, and they are worth taking one at a time.

Repeated measures / crossover
Every participant experiences every condition. Nobody is assigned to one group and compared with strangers in another group. Each person is their own control, which removes between-person variation from the comparison and makes the design dramatically more efficient than a between-groups study of the same size.
Factorial
The factors are fully crossed rather than varied one at a time. Every text colour appears in both polarities, and every one of those ten combinations appears at both light levels. This is what makes interactions estimable — you cannot detect "colour behaves differently depending on polarity" unless you have observed every colour in both polarities.
Split-plot
Not all factors sit at the same level. Polarity and colour switch within a sitting — you can change them in software in a second. Ambient illumination cannot: you cannot re-light a room mid-session without forcing the eye to re-adapt. So illumination is manipulated between sessions. It is still within-participant (everyone gets both levels), but it operates at the session level. The larger unit is the whole plot; the conditions nested inside it are the sub-plots.

Why illumination is not switched within a session

Two reasons, both practical. A fully crossed within-session design would mean twenty conditions in one sitting, which is unmanageable. And room light cannot be changed without forcing the eye to re-adapt, which would contaminate the condition that follows the switch. Sessions are separated by 48 to 72 hours, and the order of the two light levels is counterbalanced between participants so that any session-order effect cancels out across the sample.

The cost of that choice, stated in the limitations

Because illumination is manipulated between sessions rather than within, its effect is more exposed to between-session variance — time of day, sleep, circadian phase. The design mitigates this by counterbalancing order and recording covariates, but it cannot eliminate it. The synopsis says so in §4.3 rather than leaving it for an examiner to find.

Setting

A controlled laboratory room at Assam down town University in which ambient illuminance can be held constant and daylight is excluded — the last point matters, because daylight cannot be held at 10 lux. Data collection is expected to take approximately 24 months after ethics approval and piloting.

07 — The ten conditionsWhere the arithmetic does the experimental work

Covers · §3.3 Display conditions, Table 3.4 · §3.4 Ambient illumination · §1.5 Rationale

This is the technical heart of the study, and it is the section the reviewer flagged for discussion. It rewards slow reading. The claim is that a fact about arithmetic — not a clever choice of colours — creates the leverage that lets the study separate two explanations that cannot be separated experimentally.

First, how contrast is calculated

The accessibility standard defines a contrast ratio from relative luminance, written L — a number from 0 (black) to 1 (white) describing how much light a colour reflects or emits, weighted for human sensitivity. Green contributes most to it, blue least, which is why a saturated blue is far darker than a saturated yellow even when both look vivid.

The two formulae, and the whole trick

On a white background: ratio = 1.05 ÷ (L + 0.05) — this falls as the text gets lighter.

On a black background: ratio = (L + 0.05) ÷ 0.05 — this rises as the text gets lighter.

Both depend on the same single quantity, L. One is decreasing in it, the other increasing. Therefore, for any set of colours whatsoever, the ranking on white must be the exact reverse of the ranking on black. There is no colour set for which this fails.

Four properties of this matrix, and what each one buys

1 · The reversal is arithmetic, and the design says so

Covered above. What the choice of colours does control is not the reversal but two other things: how balanced the two polarities are, and whether any condition is too faint to read at all.

2 · The achromatic pair is contrast-matched — the one clean test of polarity

Black-on-white and white-on-black both give exactly 21.00 : 1. That single pair therefore changes polarity while holding luminance contrast constant. It is an experimental control rather than a statistical one, and it is the only comparison in the entire design where a polarity effect cannot be blamed on contrast.

3 · Conditions below the accessibility minimum are balanced, two per polarity

Green (3.19) and yellow (2.39) fall below AA on white; blue (3.14) and red (3.66) fall below AA on black. Because failures are split evenly, polarity is not systematically confounded with accessibility compliance. Across the whole ten-condition set the correlation between polarity and log contrast is +0.11 — close enough to zero that both can enter the same statistical model without collinearity.

4 · Michelson contrast is useless under negative polarity

The synopsis reports a second contrast metric, Michelson contrast, defined as (Lmax − Lmin) ÷ (Lmax + Lmin). On a pure black background the minimum luminance is zero, so the formula collapses to L/L = 1.000 for every single colour. Look at the Michelson column of Table 3.4: five identical values. The metric cannot discriminate between conditions it is meant to distinguish.

A small finding in its own right

This is why the accessibility contrast ratio, backed by measured display photometry, serves as the analytic contrast variable rather than Michelson. And it reinforces the study's broader argument: a luminance-insensitive metric is inadequate for dark-mode interfaces. A perceptual replacement that does incorporate polarity and typography is under development, but is not yet a standard.

Why this colour set and not another

Before the set was fixed, four candidates were compared by simulation on three criteria: whether sub-AA conditions balance across polarity, the correlation between polarity and log contrast (a confounding index where zero is ideal), and the contrast of the weakest condition.

The counter-intuitive result

Matching the colours for equal luminance — the move that sounds like the rigorous thing to do — is the worst of the four options. Equalising luminance across hues necessarily makes every chromatic colour low-contrast on white and high-contrast on black, which pushes all four sub-AA conditions into one polarity and raises the confounding correlation from +0.11 to +0.43. The intuitive fix makes the confound nearly four times worse.

Adding green to the original three was the change that balanced the sub-AA count at two per polarity. That is what the green condition is for.

Holding everything else still

  • A single fixed tablet, viewing distance 50–60 cm.
  • Brightness fixed; automatic brightness and any blue-light filter disabled; screen cleaned before each session.
  • White-point luminance measured photometrically each session, target 120–150 cd/m² — informed by an optimum of 153 cd/m² determined under 300 lux, scaled down for the lower domestic illuminance used here.
  • Typography, line height and margins identical across all conditions, so polarity and colour are the only stimulus properties that vary.

08 — How many peopleWhy 130, when the instinct says 200

Covers · §3.2 sampling, Tables 3.1–3.3 · §3.8 Counterbalancing

The formula everyone reaches for does not apply here

The familiar sample-size formula n = Z²pq/e² estimates how many people you need to pin down a prevalence in a population, assuming every observation is independent. Neither assumption holds. This is a controlled repeated-measures experiment on a purposively selected group, no prevalence is being estimated, and each participant serves as their own control — so observations within a person are deliberately not independent. Using that formula here would be a category error, and the synopsis says so explicitly.

Where the guide's instinct is right, and where it does not apply

The intuition that n should exceed 200 comes from between-participant research, where n is the number of independent replicates. That intuition is correct — for Objective 3 only. It does not transfer to the within-participant contrasts, which carry the primary outcome, because there the replicates are condition-runs and there are 2,600 of them.

Two exclusions deserve a word. Colour-vision deficiency is excluded because the study manipulates hue — a deficient observer would be receiving a different stimulus. Contact lenses on test days are excluded because lens wear independently destabilises the tear film, which is the primary outcome's own pathway.

Screening and profiling

Screening comprises visual acuity, non-cycloplegic refraction, cover test, near point of convergence, and Ishihara colour-vision screening administered on the study display itself — a method whose sensitivity was 94.4% and specificity 82.4% on a calibrated monitor, and 96.0% / 94.7% on a smartphone, with no significant difference between modes. Screening is an aid, not a diagnosis; results are recorded as covariates.

Participants also complete a profiling questionnaire covering habitual display-mode preference, screen exposure, lighting environment, familiarity with coloured interfaces, digital literacy, hours since waking and recent caffeine intake. The first four supply the Objective 3 moderators; the last two are sleepiness covariates.

Sampling honesty

Recruitment is non-probability purposive (convenience) sampling, with consecutive enrolment of eligible volunteers from the university and associated institutions. This is a deliberate choice: it keeps cognitive, visual and digital-exposure characteristics reasonably homogeneous, so the within-subject contrasts are not diluted by uncontrolled heterogeneity. The cost is stated plainly — the findings are not intended to estimate population prevalence, and generalisation beyond comparable young-adult screen users is made with caution.

Controlling for order — the Williams square

Two order-related threats arise when one person sees ten conditions in a row:

  • Serial-position effects — the vigilance decrement and accumulating fatigue mean that whichever condition comes tenth will look worse than it is.
  • First-order carryover — notably the light-adaptation transient imposed by a polarity switch, which contaminates the condition that follows it.

A balanced Williams-type Latin square over the ten conditions handles both at once, because across a complete block it guarantees two properties simultaneously:

1. Every condition occupies every serial position equally often — so position effects cancel.

2. Every condition precedes and follows every other condition equally often — so carryover cancels.

Participants are assigned to rows by sequential enrolment index, and complete balance is achieved in blocks of ten. This is the real reason the sample size is a multiple of ten: 130 is 13 complete blocks. Illumination order is counterbalanced orthogonally on top of that, so dim-first and moderate-first are equally represented within each block.

09 — Inside a sessionWhat a participant actually does

Covers · §3.6 Procedure, Figure 3.3 · §3.5 instrumentation

The session frame

  1. 1Setup, seating and calibrationViewing distance set to 50–60 cm. Per-participant calibration establishes an open-eye baseline, a nine-point gaze mapping and a frontal head-pose reference — so every metric is referenced to this person rather than to population defaults.
  2. 2Baseline CVS-Q and fatigue VASRecorded before any reading, so the key secondary outcome is a change score rather than an absolute one.
  3. 3Ten display conditions, in Williams orderEach condition runs the full seven-step sequence below. A self-paced rest break follows every two conditions.
  4. 4CVS-Q repeated, NASA-TLX administered onceThe questionnaire is repeated to give the change score; workload is captured once per session as a cumulative index rather than after every condition.

The seven steps inside every single condition

  1. iAdaptation on a neutral grey field60 seconds, extended to 120 seconds when the polarity switches. This is the carryover control from Part 08 made physical — the eye is given time to re-adapt before the next condition is measured.
  2. iiReading taskA standardised passage in the active condition, self-paced beyond a per-page minimum. This is the exposure window from which the ocular metrics are taken — the primary outcome comes from here and nowhere else.
  3. iiiComprehensionMultiple-choice items assessing gist, inference and detail — three levels, so a ceiling on one does not hide effects on another.
  4. ivDisplay perceptionComfort and clarity ratings, 0–100, captured immediately after reading while the impression is fresh.
  5. vVisual-fatigue VASFive items, 0–10, per condition.
  6. viVisual searchTap every occurrence of a target word, fixed time limit.
  7. viiGo/no-go reaction timeA dot appears at random screen locations on the active background. Yields reaction-time mean, SD and coefficient of variation, lapse rate, anticipations, and signal-detection d′ and criterion.
A design detail worth noticing

The reaction-time target is achromatic and defined relative to the active background — black on light fields, white on dark ones — with the chromatic set used as distractors. So target contrast is maximal and identical in both polarities, salience does not vary with condition, and no target hue coincides with the text-colour manipulation.

The discrimination becomes achromatic-against-chromatic rather than hue-against-hue. That simplifies the task, but it preserves the two indices that are actually fatigue-sensitive: reaction-time variability and lapse rate.

Three more controls

  • Passages matched for genre, length and readability, and assigned counterbalanced independently of condition — so passage difficulty is orthogonal to display condition rather than confounded with it.
  • No performance feedback is given, so effort is not differentially modulated across conditions.
  • Rest break every two conditions, self-paced.
The pilot and the feasibility gate — §3.6

Ten participants precede the main study. Three pre-specified criteria decide whether the two-session structure survives:

· median session duration ≤ 120 minutes
· no more than one withdrawal in ten
· face-presence ratio ≥ 0.90 in at least 90% of condition-runs

If any criterion fails, each illumination level is split into two shorter sittings of five conditions, preserving the counterbalancing scheme and the global serial-position record. The fallback is specified in advance rather than improvised, which is what makes it a gate rather than a hope.

10 — Measuring the eyeHow a tablet camera counts an incomplete blink

Covers · §3.5 Instrumentation · operational definitions · validation sub-study

Eye aspect ratio, in one paragraph

Facial-landmark tracking places a set of points around each eye on every processed video frame. From those points a single scalar is computed — the eye aspect ratio, or EAR — which is essentially the height of the eye opening divided by its width. When the eye is open the number is high; as the lid descends it falls toward zero. A blink is not detected as an image; it is detected as a temporal pattern in this one number.

Two properties make it right for this job. It is continuous, so it registers partial closure rather than only a binary open/shut. And it is normalised, so it can be referenced to each participant's own open-eye baseline instead of to a population value — which matters because eye shape varies enormously between people.

Stated as provisional, on purpose

The synopsis says the 0.75 and 0.60 thresholds are provisional and will be finalised against manually annotated video in the validation subsample before the main analysis is run. Fixing them afterwards, once outcomes were visible, would be a form of analytic flexibility that could manufacture a result.

Quality control as data, not as deletion

The application records achieved sampling rate, face-presence ratio, frame luminance, head stability and flags for disengagement — and keeps them as variables rather than using them to silently bin data. Three consequences:

  • Duration-dependent blink metrics are gated automatically on the achieved sampling rate, because below roughly 25 fps a 100 ms event is not reliably timed.
  • Every measure is written to storage immediately, so an interruption loses at most the condition in progress.
  • All camera processing happens on the device and no video is retained — except for the consented validation segments.

Declaring one primary outcome in advance is what prevents the study from testing thirty things and reporting whichever three came out significant. Everything else is explicitly supportive.

The confirmatory model, piece by piece

Fixed effects — what is being estimated

Polarity · text colour · ambient illumination · all two-way interactions · the three-way interaction · serial position · illumination order.

Serial position and illumination order are in the model because Part 08's counterbalancing removes their bias but not their variance — modelling them recovers precision.

Why the outcome is modelled on the logit scale

The incomplete-blink ratio is a bounded proportion: it cannot go below 0 or above 1. Treating it as an ordinary linear outcome would let the model predict impossible values, and would assume constant variance when in fact variance is smallest near the bounds. So it is modelled on the logit scale, or with a beta or binomial mixed model. This is not fussiness — it is the difference between a model that can and cannot represent the data.

Why the random structure has two levels

This is the split-plot design from Part 06 expressed in statistical form:

  • a random intercept for participant — people differ in their baseline blink behaviour;
  • a random intercept for session nested within participant — the same person differs between their two sessions, and illumination is a session-level factor;
  • a random slope for polarity where the data support it — people may differ in how much polarity affects them, which is itself the Objective 3 question.
The convergence contingency, pre-specified

Maximal random structures often fail to converge. Rather than improvising, the reduction order is fixed in advance — random slopes first, then the session intercept — and whatever reduction was needed is reported. This closes off a route by which a researcher could try structures until one gave a favourable answer.

How Objective 2 is actually tested

Two nested model families are fitted and compared:

ModelColour enters as…What it represents
CategoricalA five-level factor, each colour free to have its own effectThe saturated description — colour matters, for whatever reason
Contrast + residualLuminance contrast as a continuous predictor, with colour retained to pick up whatever contrast fails to explainThe mechanistic account — contrast does the work, hue is a leftover

Comparative fit, and the size of the residual hue terms, quantify how much of the colour effect is really a contrast effect. If contrast explains it all, the residual hue terms shrink toward zero.

The remaining analyses

  • Objective 3 (moderation) — profiling variables entered as between-participant terms with condition-by-moderator interactions.
  • Objective 4 (validation) — as specified in Part 10.
  • VAS and Likert outcomes — ordinal cumulative-link models, because a 0–10 rating is not an interval scale.
  • Accuracy outcomes — binomial generalised mixed models.
  • Signal-detection indices (d′, criterion) — treated hierarchically, because they are unstable at small per-condition trial counts.
  • Sensitivity analyses — models re-fitted (a) excluding conditions flagged for disengagement or poor data quality, and (b) with PERCLOS as a covariate, which is how visual fatigue is separated from plain sleepiness.

Residual diagnostics, multiplicity control within outcome families, and effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals are reported throughout. The three-way interaction is treated as exploratory, because Part 08 showed it has the least power.

15 — GlossaryEvery term, alphabetically

Accommodation
The eye changing the shape of its internal lens to focus on something near. Effortful to sustain; contributes to the focusing route of visual fatigue. Not measured in this study.
Achromatic
Having no hue — black, white or grey. The achromatic conditions (P1, N1) are the study's contrast-matched anchor pair.
APCA — Advanced Perceptual Contrast Algorithm
A candidate successor to the current accessibility contrast metric that does incorporate polarity and typographic parameters. Under development, not yet a standard. Cited as evidence that the polarity-blindness of the current criterion is recognised.
Bland–Altman
A method for comparing two measurement techniques by plotting their difference against their average, reporting bias and 95% limits of agreement. Used to compare the automated incomplete-blink ratio against manual annotation.
Carryover
Contamination of one condition by the condition that preceded it. Here the specific worry is the light-adaptation transient after a polarity switch — handled by the Williams square and by extending adaptation to 120 s at a switch.
Cohen's κ (kappa)
An agreement statistic for categorical judgements that corrects for chance agreement. The study requires κ ≥ 0.60 between its two human annotators before their coding is used as a standard.
Condition-run
One participant experiencing one display condition once. The unit that governs precision for within-participant contrasts. This study produces 2,600 of them.
Contrast ratio (WCAG)
The accessibility standard's contrast measure, computed from relative luminance: (Llighter + 0.05) ÷ (Ldarker + 0.05). Ranges from 1:1 to 21:1. Thresholds: 3:1 (AA Large), 4.5:1 (AA), 7:1 (AAA). Takes no account of text size, weight or polarity.
Counterbalancing
Systematically varying the order in which conditions are presented across participants so that order effects cancel rather than accumulate in one direction.
Crossover design
Every participant receives every condition, in a counterbalanced order, serving as their own control.
CVS-Q — Computer Vision Syndrome Questionnaire
A validated 16-symptom instrument scoring frequency and intensity on a Rasch-fitting scale, with a published cut-off of 7. Change from session baseline is this study's key secondary outcome.
d′ (d-prime) and criterion
Signal-detection measures separating a participant's genuine sensitivity (d′) from their willingness to respond (criterion). Derived from the go/no-go task; modelled hierarchically because they are unstable at low trial counts.
d_z
The effect size for a within-participant comparison: the mean of the paired differences divided by the standard deviation of those differences. Distinct from a between-groups d — a d_z of 0.25 is detectable with far fewer people than a between-groups d of 0.25.
Digital eye strain / computer vision syndrome
Two names for the same condition: the cluster of ocular and visual symptoms attributable to prolonged display use. Pooled prevalence 69.0%.
EAR — eye aspect ratio
A single number per video frame describing how open the eye is, derived from facial landmarks. Continuous and normalised, which is what lets it distinguish partial from complete lid closure.
Factorial design
All factors fully crossed, so every level of one appears with every level of the others. Necessary to estimate interactions.
Incomplete blink
A blink in which the lids approach but never fully meet, leaving part of the ocular surface unwetted. Operationally: minimum EAR stays between 0.60 and 0.75 of the participant's baseline.
Incomplete-blink ratio (IBR)
Incomplete blinks ÷ all detected blinks, within a condition. The study's primary outcome.
Logit scale
The transformation log[p ÷ (1 − p)], which maps a proportion bounded at 0 and 1 onto the whole real line. Used so the model cannot predict impossible values for the incomplete-blink ratio.
Luminance / relative luminance
Luminance is emitted light, in cd/m². Relative luminance is a 0–1 normalised version weighted for human sensitivity (green weighted most, blue least) — the single input to both contrast formulae.
Luminance contrast
The brightness difference between text and background. Distinct from colour difference, and the property that actually governs legibility.
Michelson contrast
(Lmax − Lmin) ÷ (Lmax + Lmin). Reported for completeness but analytically useless in negative polarity, where a pure black background drives it to 1.000 for every colour.
Mixed-effects model
A regression containing both fixed effects (the factors of interest) and random effects (participant- and session-level variation). Required here because observations are nested inside people and sessions rather than independent.
Moderation
When the size of an effect depends on a characteristic of the person. Objective 3. Tested between participants, which is why it is the power-hungry analysis.
NASA-TLX
A six-dimension self-report index of cognitive workload. Administered once per session.
Near-work-induced transient myopia
A temporary shift towards short-sightedness after sustained close work. A symptom of the focusing route.
Negative polarity
Light text on a dark background. Dark mode.
Ocular surface
The exposed front of the eye, kept comfortable by the tear film and renewed by complete blinking. The route this study measures.
Orthogonal
Statistically independent — two variables that carry non-overlapping information and can therefore both enter a model. Polarity and log contrast are near-orthogonal here (r = +0.11).
PERCLOS
The proportion of time the eyes are more than 80% closed. A validated drowsiness index, used here strictly as a sleepiness covariate because it is insensitive in moderate drowsiness.
Positive polarity
Dark text on a light background. The arrangement of a printed book.
Positive polarity advantage
The well-replicated finding that reading performance is better in positive than negative polarity, attributed to a mostly-white screen emitting more light, constricting the pupil and improving retinal image quality.
Power
The probability of detecting an effect of a given size if it is really there. Conventionally 80% is the minimum acceptable. Estimated here by simulation rather than formula.
Purposive (convenience) sampling
Non-probability recruitment of accessible eligible volunteers. Chosen deliberately for homogeneity; the cost is that prevalence cannot be estimated and generalisation is limited.
Serial position effect
Systematic differences caused by where in the sequence a condition fell rather than by the condition itself. Handled by the Williams square and modelled as a covariate.
Split-plot
A design in which factors operate at different levels — here illumination at session level (whole plot) and polarity × colour within session (sub-plot). Its statistical signature is two nested random intercepts.
sRGB linearisation
Undoing the non-linear encoding of standard display colour values before computing luminance. Without this step contrast calculations are simply wrong.
Tear film
The thin fluid layer covering the ocular surface, renewed by each complete blink and capped by an oily layer that slows evaporation. Its breakdown is the mechanism behind display-associated dry eye.
Veiling luminance
Stray ambient light reflecting off the screen surface, which raises the darkest achievable black and so reduces effective contrast. One of the two ways room light acts.
Vergence
Both eyes turning inward so their two images fuse into one. Effortful at reading distance; part of the focusing route. Not measured here.
Williams Latin square
A counterbalancing scheme in which, across a complete block, every condition occupies every serial position equally often and precedes and follows every other condition equally often. Complete balance for ten conditions needs blocks of ten — which is why n is a multiple of ten.
